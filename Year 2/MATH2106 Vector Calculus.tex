\subsubsection{Integral transform example}
Find the area of the region \(R\) defined by:
\[x\leq y^2\leq 2x,\qquad y\leq x^2\leq 2y\]
Let \(u=x^2/y\) and \(v=y^2/x\). Then:
\begin{align*}
    &y^2=2x&\implies &v=2 \\
    &y^2=x&\implies &v=1 \\
    &y=x^2&\implies&u=1 \\
    &y=x^2/2&\implies&u=2
\end{align*}
We know that:
\[\iint_R\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y=\iint_{S}|\underline{\underline{J}}|\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\]
Where \(|\underline{\underline{J}}|\) is the determinant of the Jacobian matrix. However it is often easier to work with \(|\underline{\underline{J}}^{-1}|\), where we have the result:
\[|\underline{\underline{J}}^{-1}|=\left|\pf{(u,v)}{(x,y)}\right|=\left|\begin{pmatrix}
    \pf{u}{x} & \pf{u}{y} \\ \pf{v}{x} & \pf{v}{y}
\end{pmatrix}\right|=|\underline{\underline{J}}|^{-1}\]

\subsection{Scalar and Vector Fields}
In an applied maths setting, fields can be thought of as functions of position. If the resulting quantity is a scalar we have a scalar field and similarly with a vector. An example might be a terrain map, consider the height \(h=h(x,y)\) above a terrain. This is clearly a scalar field and is denoted as such. Now consider the slope of all points on the terrain:
\[\underline{s}=\underline{s}(x,y)=\left(\pf{h}{x},\,\pf{h}{y}\right)\]
This is a vector field and is denoted with an underline to show that. We can obviously think of examples for many dimensions, but this module primarily focuses on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

\subsection{Gradient of a Scalar Field}
Consider a scalar field \(f(x,y,z)\). It's gradient is denoted \(\underline{\nabla}f\) and is the vector-valued functions:
\[\grad{f}=\left(\pf{f}{x},\,\pf{f}{y},\,\pf{f}{z}\right)\]
It's direction is that of the fastest increase of \(f\) and its magnitude is the size of this increase. It is orthogonal to the level surfaces of \(f\). Because it is a vector, we can project it in any direction we please and we call this the directional derivative:
\[\underline{\hat{u}}\cdot\grad{f}\]
Physically, this represents a normal vector to the curve/surface etc, which can then be normalised:
\[\underline{\hat{n}}=\frac{\grad{f}}{\|\grad{f}\|}\]
It is convenient to consider the gradient of \(f\) as the action of an "operator" \(\grad{}\) onto a field \(f\):
\[\grad{}=\left(\pf{}{x},\,\pf{}{y}\,,\pf{}{z}\right)=\underline{i}\pf{}{x}+\underline{j}\pf{}{y}+\underline{k}\pf{}{z}\]
\subsubsection{Divergence of a vector field}
We can also apply the gradient operator to a vector field through means of a dot product. Consider the vector field \(\underline{A}=(A_x,A_y,A_z)\) and then construct:
\[\dive{A}=\pf{A_x}{x}+\pf{A_y}{y}+\pf{A_z}{z}=\text{div} \underline{A}\]
The divergence of a vector field \(A\) describes flux through a closed surface. It is also a vector field which shows if the underlying field is expanding (a positive div) or contracting (a negative div). The field is called solenoidal in a region if it's divergence there is exactly 0.
\subsubsection*{Worked example 1}
Find \(\underline{\hat{n}}\) to \(f=x^2+y^2-z\) at \((1,1,2)\).
\[\grad{f}=(2x,2y,-1)\]
\[\grad{f}(1,1,2)=(2,2,-1)\]
\[\|\grad{f}\|=3\]
\[\underline{\hat{n}}=\frac{\grad{f}}{\|\grad{f}\|}=\frac{1}{3}(2,2,-1)\]
\subsubsection*{Worked example 2}
Find the directional derivative at the origin of \(f=2x+y+z^2\) in the direction \((1,1,1)\)
\[\underline{u}=(1,1,1)\implies \underline{\hat{u}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)\]
\[\underline{\hat{u}}\cdot\grad{f}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,\,1,\,1)\cdot(2,\,1,\,2z)\]
\[\underline{\hat{u}}\cdot\grad{f}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{2z}{\sqrt{3}}=\frac{2z+3}{\sqrt3}\]
Hence at the origin, the directional derivative is:
\[\frac{3}{\sqrt{3}}\]
\subsubsection{Curl of a vector field}
Exactly as we did with the scalar product, we can take a cross product of the gradient operator and a vector field \(\underline{A}=(A_x,A_y,A_z)\). This works exactly as you would expect although with some horrendous notation abuse:
\[\curl{A}=\det\begin{pmatrix}
    \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\
    \partial x & \partial y & \partial z \\
    A_x & A_y & A_z
\end{pmatrix}\]
\[=\underline{i}\left(\pf{A_z}{y}-\pf{A_y}{z}\right)-\underline{j}\left(\pf{A_z}{x}-\pf{A_x}{z}\right)+\underline{k}\left(\pf{A_y}{x}-\pf{A_x}{y}\right)\]
The curl describes the "rotation" or "twisting" of the underlying vector field. A field is called irrotational if it's curl is \(0\).
\subsubsection*{Worked Example}
Let \(\underline{A}=(x,y)\). Then:
\[\curl{A}=\det\begin{pmatrix}
    \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\
    \partial x& \partial y & \partial z \\
    x & y & 0
\end{pmatrix}= \underline{i}(0-0)-\underline{j}(0-0)+\underline{k}(0-0)=\underline{0}\]
\subsection{The Laplacian}
Consider taking the divergence of the gradient of a vector field. This is a perfectly mathematically acceptable operation and in fact has some important uses.:
\[\dive{\nabla}f=\pf{^2f}{x^2}+\pf{^2f}{y^2}+\pf{^2f}{z^2}=\nabla^2f\]
This is a scalar field! We can consider \(\nabla^2\) as an operator in it's own right and apply it to vector fields. This is an interesting property because it means that the operator can be used on both scalars and vectors in a very similar way.
\begin{align*}
    &\underline{u}=(u_1,u_2,u_3,\dots) \\
    &\nabla^2\underline{u}=(\nabla^2u_1,\,\nabla^2u_2,\,\nabla^2u_3,\dots)
\end{align*}
Where each component is the Laplacian. The physical meaning of the Laplacian relates to diffusion - consider the divergence being a measure of if the vector field is expanding, and the Laplacian as a measure of how quickly it is expanding. The Laplacian is very important and comes up all the time: In particular it is important for diffusion equations such as the heat equation.
\[\pf{u}{t}=D\pf{^2u}{x^2}\]
\begin{align*}
    &u(x,0)=f(x):=\text{hat function} \\
    &u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0
\end{align*}
\subsection{Other Operations Involving \(\nabla\)}
\subsubsection{The operator \(\underline{A}\cdot \underline{\nabla}\)}
For any vector or vector field \(\underline{A}\), we can define \(\underline{A}\cdot \underline{\nabla}\). This is a new operator that can be applied to scalars or vectors:
\[(\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\nabla})f=\left(A_x\pf{}{x}+A_y\pf{}{y}+A_z\pf{}{z}\right)f\]
\[(\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\nabla})\underline{B}=\left(A_x\pf{\underline{B}}{x}+A_y\pf{\underline{B}}{y}+A_z\pf{\underline{B}}{z}\right)\]
\subsubsection{The curl of a gradient vector field}
The curl of a gradient vector field vanishes:
\[\underline{\nabla}\times (\grad{f})=\underline{0}\]
We say that a gradient field is necessarily irrotational. The converse of this statement is also true: if a vector field is irrotational, it can be written as the gradient of a scalar field - this result is helpful later in this module.
\subsubsection{Divergence of a curl vector field}
The divergence of a curl vector field vanishes:
\[\underline{\nabla}\cdot(\curl{A})=0\]
We say that curl vector fields are necessarily solenoidal. The converse is also true: if the divergence of a vector field is \(0\), then it can be written as a curl vector field.
\subsubsection{Curl of a curl vector field}
Since the curl of a vector field is its self a vector field, we can apply the curl operator to it again. After some messy calculations we get the following identity:
\[\underline{\nabla}\times (\curl{A})=\underline{\nabla}(\dive{A})=-\nabla^2\underline{A}\]

\section{Line, Surface, and Volume Integrals}
For most applications, line and surface integrals involve vector fields, while volume integrals are usually of scalar fields.
\subsection{Line Integrals}
\subsubsection{Ordinary definite integral}
Think of the definition of an ordinary definite integral from calculus, taking the integral as the limit of infinitesimal areas.
\[\int^b_af(x)\,\text{d}x:=\lim_{N\to \infty}\sum_if(x_i)\Delta x_i\]
Obviously we do not use this definition to actually compute the integral, but it helps us understand what is happening conceptually.
\subsubsection{Line integrals}
We can apply the same sort of logic to line integrals. Lets start with \(C\in\mathbb{R}^3\), being an oriented curve, so that a direction of travel along the curve is specified. We will split the curve into small intervals, labelling each one with the index \(i=0,\dots,N\). If we let \(\underline{r}_i\) be the vector to the starting point of the  \(i^{th}\) interval. Let \(\underline{r}_{i+1}-\underline{r}_i=\underline{\text{d}r_i}\) be the vector pointing from the start of the \(i^{th}\) interval, to it's end. As our chosen intervals get smaller and smaller, the \(\underline{\text{d}r_i}\) get closer and closer to being tangential to \(C\). We can then define the line integral of the vector field \(\underline{A}(\underline{r})\) along the curve \(C\) as the following limit:
\[\int_C\underline{A}(\underline{r})\cdot \underline{\text{d}r}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_i\underline{A}(\underline{r}_i)\cdot \underline{\text{d}r_i}\]
\subsubsection{Parametrisation}
Like before, we do not actually use this definition to compute a line integral. The actual practical prescription works like this: We need to describe \(C\) as a parametrised curve in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). All points \(\underline{r}\in C\) on the curve should be represented as:
\[C=\{\underline{r}(t):t\in[a,b]\}\]
where \(t\) is called the parameter along the curve. Note that their are many parametrisations that can be chosen for the curve, so we should aim to pick the one that makes further calculation as easy as possible for us.
\subsubsection*{Example 1}
Consider the curve that is the equation for a circle on the \((x,y,0)\) coordinate space:
\[x^2+y^2=1\]
The simplest choice for parametrising this curve which also makes it oriented is:
\[x=\cos{t},\quad y=\sin{t},\quad t\in[0,2\pi)\]
As the choice for \(t\) moves towards \(2\pi\) from \(0\), the circle is traversed anti-clockwise.
\subsubsection*{Example 2}
Consider a helix. A simple parametrisation is given by:
\[x=R\cos{t},\quad y=R\sin{t},\quad z=at,\quad t\in(0,\infty)\]
Where \(R\) and \(a\) are parameters of the helix and \(t\) is the parameter along the curve. We could have chosen any domain for \(t\), and this parameter also orients the curve.

    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/helix_r3.png}
    \caption{A helix, constructed using the above parametrisation}
\end{figure}
\subsection{Line Integral as an Ordinary Definite Integral}
Once we have chosen an appropriate parametrisation, we can compute the line integral of a vector field as follows:
\[\int_C\underline{A}(\underline{r})\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\int^{t_{max}}_{t_{min}}\underline{A}(\underline{r}(t))\cdot\df{\underline{r}(t)}{t}\,\text{d}t\]
In this equation, \(\underline{A}(\underline{r}(t))\) is the value of the vector field along the curve \(C\). The object \(\frac{\underline{\text{d}r}(t)}{dt}\) is another vector field the curve. If \(\underline{r}(t)\) defines a position, then \(\underline{\text{d}r}(t)/dt\) defines a velocity. This equation is independent of how we choose to parametrise the curve, which makes the quantity it computes - the line integral of a vector field - well defined.
\subsubsection{Line integral example}
Consider a curve \(C\), represented in parametrised form as:
\[x=t,\quad y=t,\quad z=2t^2,\quad t\in[0,1]\]
Consider the vector field \(\underline{A}=(y,z,x)\). We wish to compute the line integral of \(\underline{A}\) along the curve.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/lineintegral1.png}
    \caption{The curve \(C\) over the vector field \(A\)}
\end{figure}
We first compute the velocity vector field along the curve:
\[\df{\underline{r}(t)}{t}=\left(x'(t),\,y'(t),\,z'(t)\right)=(1,1,4t)\]
We now need to evaluate the vector field at each point:
\[\underline{A}(\underline{r}(t))=(t,\,2t^2,\,t)\]
We can now compute the line integral:
\[\int_C\underline{A}(\underline{r})\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\int^1_0(t,2t^2,t)\cdot(1,1,4t)\,\text{d}t\]
\[=\int^1_0(t+6t^2)=6\]
\subsubsection{Choose parametrisation example}
In the above example, we were given a curve already parametrised. In this example, the curve will be given geometrically and we will need to choose our own parametrisation. Let \(\underline{A}=(y,x,z)\). Let \(C\) be the curve that connects the origin to the point \((1,2,3)\). The simplest parametrisation we can make is:
\[\underline{r}(t)=(1,2,3),\quad t\in[0,1]\]
Then the velocity vector is a constant vector in the direction of the line: \(\underline{r}'(t)=(1,2,3)\). The vector field along the curve is given by:
\[\underline{A}(\underline{r}(t))=(2t,t,3t)\]
Then the line integral is:
\[\int^1_0(2t,t,3t)\cdot(1,2,3)\,\text{d}t=\int^1_013t\,\text{d}t=\frac{13}{2}\]
\subsubsection{Closed curve line integral example}
Let us integrate the vector field \(\underline{A}=(y,x,z)\) along a closed curve, which is the unit circle in the \(x - y\) plane. We will parametrise the circle in the simplest possible way:
\[\underline{r}(t)=(\cos(t),\,\sin(t),\,0),\quad t\in[0,2\pi]\]
The velocity vector field is then:
\[\df{\underline{r}}{t}=(-\sin(t),\,\cos(t),\,0)\]
The vector field along the curve is \(\underline{A}(\underline{r}(t))=(\sin(t),\,\cos(t),\,0)\). This means that the line integral is:
\[\oint \underline{A}\cdot \underline{\text{d}r}=\int^{2\pi}_0-\sin^2(t)+\cos^2(t)\,\text{d}t=\int^{2\pi}_0\cos(2t)\,\text{d}t=0\]
Note that we have used a new integral symbol. This is simply to note that we are integrating along a closed curve. An integral of this type is often called circulation for the vector field. Vector fields with 0 circulation will become important later.

\subsection{Surface Integrals}
\subsubsection{Year 1 revision}
Recall from last year that surface integrals can be defined by the way of a limiting process:
\[\iint f(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_i f(x_i,y_i)\Delta A_i\]
This is not how we actually compute them though:
\[\iint_Df(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y=\int^d_c\left\{\int^{S_+(y)}_{S_-(y)}f(x,y)\,\text{d}x\right\}\,\text{d}y\]
It is often convenient to change coordinate systems to make this integral easier to compute.
\subsubsection{Motivating examples and a word on other integrals}
Example problems that make use of surface integrals relevant to this module include: fluid flowing through a structure / flux through a surface. Similar to line integrals, there are many other surface integrals that we can take although the standard dot product one is generally seen as the most physically useful. That being said any valid integral you can construct you can also compute. Similar to line integrals, we have a new notation for closed surface integrals:
\[\oiint_S\underline{F}\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S\]
\subsection{Computing surface integrals}
How do we actually compute surface integrals? Like line integrals, we need to parametrise the surface so that we can express the integral:
\[\iint_S\underline{u}(\underline{r}(x,y))\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S\]
as the integral:
\[\iint \underline{u}(\underline{r}(u,v))\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\]
Where the normal to the surface \(\underline{n}\) is given by:
\[\pf{\underline{r}}{u}\times \pf{\underline{r}}{v}\]
\subsubsection{Standard transform example}
Compute the integral \(\iint_S\underline{A}\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S\), of the vector field \(\underline{A}=(x,\,z,\,-y)\) over the curved surface of the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=1\) between \(z=0\) and \(z=1\) oriented with the normal facing outwards.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/cylindernormal.png}
    \caption{The cylinder in question, marked with the normals that we are considering.}
\end{figure}
We know that an appropriate parametrisation would be putting the surface into cylindrical coordinates:
\[\begin{cases}
    x=\rho\cos\phi \\
    y=\rho \sin\phi \\
    z=z
\end{cases}\]
On our surface we have \(\rho=1,\ \phi\in[0,2\pi),\ z\in[0,1]\). Our surface can be given in cylindrical coordinates as:
\[\underline{r}(\phi,z)=(\cos\phi,\, \sin\phi,\, z)\]
Since we want our normal to be tangential to the plane:
\[\underline{n}=\pf{\underline{r}}{\phi}\times\pf{\underline{r}}{z}=\pm((-\sin\phi,\, \cos\phi,\,0)\times (0,0,1)) \]
We take the positive sign since we require our normal be pointing outwards. You can always check what direction your normals are pointing by substituting in an appropriate point on the surface. Finally we can do our surface integral:
\[\iint_S\underline{A}\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S=\int^{2\pi}_0\int^1_0\underline{A}(\underline{r})\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}z\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[\underline{A}(\underline{r})=(\cos\phi,\, z,\, -\sin\phi)\]
\[\text{d}S=\rho \text{d}\phi\text{d}z\]
\[\int^{2\pi}_0\int^1_0(\cos\phi,\, z,\, -\sin\phi)\cdot (\cos\phi,\, \sin\phi,\, 0)\,\text{d}z\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[=\int^{2\pi}_0\left[z\cos^2\phi+\frac{z^2}{2}\sin\phi\right]^1_0\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[=\left[\frac{1}{4}\sin2\phi+\frac{\phi}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos\phi \right]^{2\pi}_0=\pi\]
Notice that here we made the standard integral transform from Cartesian to cylindrical coordinates. This transform is given in the formula sheet but if you need to derive it, then it comes from the Jacobian.
\subsubsection{Non-standard transform example}
Find the surface integral of \(\underline{A}=\underline{r}\) over \(z=1-x^2-y^2\) with \(z>0\) and the normal pointing outwards.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/parabaloid.png}
    \caption{We are only considering the paraboloid where \(z>0\)}
\end{figure}
Note that there doesn't seem to be an obvious parametrisation that we can make so we will just leave it in \(x,y\):
\[\underline{r}=(x,y,1-x^2-y^2);\quad x^2+y^2\leq 1\]
We need to find our \(\underline{n}\):
\[\pf{\underline{r}}{x}\times \pf{\underline{r}}{y}=(1,0,-2x)\times(0,1,-2y)\]
\[\underline{n}=\pm(2x,2y,1)\]
Since we require the outward normal we have \(\underline{n}=(2x,2y,1)\). Then:
\[I=\iint_S\underline{A}\cdot \underline{n}\,\text{d}S=\iint_S(x,y,1-x^2-y^2)\cdot (2x,2y,1)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\iint_Sx^2+y^2+1\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
Now we can see an appropriate transform to make our integrating domain simpler - we can convert to polar coordinates.
\[=\int^{2\pi}_{0}\int^1_0\rho(\rho^2+1)\,\text{d}\rho\,\text{d}\phi\]
\subsection{Volume integrals}
Consider a volume \(V\) and a density \(\rho(\underline{r})\). If we split the object into sufficiently small 'volume elements' then the density in that region will be approximately constant. In this tune we can think of the mass of the object as the result of a limiting process:
\[M_i=\sum^N_{i=1}\rho(\underline{r}_i)\delta V_i\]
\[M=\iiint_V\rho \,\text{d}V=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}\rho(\underline{r}_i)\delta V_-i\]
Often we will only write one integral sign since the dimension is implied by the domain of integration, this can tidy up our notation somewhat. Similar to surface and line integrals we need a new method to actually compute these integrals. This is exactly as you would expect and follows the same procedure from year 1 analysis.
\[\int _Vf(\underline{r})\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}z=\int_{V'}f(\underline{u}(u,v,w))\left|\pf{(x,y,z)}{(u,v,w)}\right|\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\,\text{d}w\]
This is exactly what performing a surface integral looked like, here we just have an extra dimension.
\subsubsection{Unit sphere example}
Find the volume of the unit sphere.
\[x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi\]
\[y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi\]
\[z=r\cos\theta\]
\[r\in[0,1] \quad \theta\in[0,\pi] \quad \phi\in[0,2\pi)\]
Then via the Jacobian we can see:
\[\text{d}V=\text{d}x\text{d}y\text{d}z=r^2\sin\theta\, \text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[\text{Volume}=\iiint_V\text{d}V=\iiint_V\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}z\]
\[=\int^1_0\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0r^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\phi\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}r\]
\subsubsection{Cube example}
Consider a solid cube bounded by \(0\leq x,y\leq 1)\) with density function \(\rho=1+xyz\). The mass of this cube is given by:
\[M=\iiint_V(1+xyz)\,\text{d}V\]
\[M=\int^1_0\int^1_0\int^1_0(1+xyz)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}z\]
\subsection{Coordinate-free Div and Curl}
Recall that the divergence measures the flux of a vector field through a surface. It can then be written as a surface integral:
\[\dive{A}:=\lim_{\delta V\to 0}\frac{1}{\delta V}\oiint_{\delta S}\underline{A}\cdot \underline{n}\,\text{d}S \]
The curl describes rotation around an axis - we define this by way of a line integral around a closed surface:
\[\underline{n}\cdot (\curl{A}):=\lim_{\delta Ss\to 0}\frac{1}{\delta S}\oint_{\delta S}\underline{A}\cdot \underline{\text{d}r}\]
Similar to a ll of the theory we have covered so far in this module, these definitions are not useful for actually computing values but rather are just there for completeness. These definitions may be assessed and are useful because they provide a coordinate free description of both div and curl.
\subsection{Conservative Vector Fields}
A vector field \(\underline{A}\) is conservative if:
\[\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot \underline{\text{d}r}=0\]
Under some suitable \(C\). These conditions are generally that \(C\) is smooth and closed although more specific conditions may apply - these are not stated in the complete lecture notes. Equivalently, a vector field is conservative if it's line integral is path-independent:
\[\int_{C_1}\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\int_{C_2}\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
We can show that we can construct the path independence property from the circulation property. Let \(\underline{A}=\underline{\nabla}f\), then:
\[\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\int_C\grad{f}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
\[=\int^{t_{max}}_{t_{min}}\grad{f}\cdot \df{\underline{r}}{t}\text{d}t=\int^{t_{max}}_{t_{min}}\df{}{t}\left(f(\underline{r}(t))\right)\]
\[=f(\underline{r}(t_{max}))-f(\underline{r}(t_{min}))\]
Clearly if the circulation is 0, then we have the scalar field evaluated at the endpoints to be the same:
\[f(\underline{r}(t_{max}))=f(\underline{r}(t_{min}))\]
\subsubsection{Example applying conservative properties} 
Consider the vector field \(\underline{A}=(x^2,y^2)\) and the curve:
\[C=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=1,\ y\geq 0\}\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/conservativefields.png}
    \caption{The curve \(C\), over the vector field \(A\)}
\end{figure}
We can choose an appropriate parametrisation (in this case, polar) and find that:
\[\underline{r}(t)=(\cos(t),\sin(t))\implies \underline{r}'(t)=(-\sin(t),\cos(t))\]
\[\underline{A}(\underline{r}(t))=(\cos^2(t),\,\sin^2(t))\]
\[I=\int^\pi_0(\cos^2(t),\, \sin^2(t))\cdot (-\sin(t),\,\cos(t))\,\text{d}t=-\frac{2}{3}\]
Is there an easier way to solve this problem? Yes there is! Notice that:
\[\curl{A}=0\iff \exists\,\phi:\; \underline{A}=\grad{\phi}\]
We can find this \(\phi\) by an algorithmic approach or, in this case, by inspection.
\[\phi=\left(\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{y^3}{3}\right)\]
Since we have \(\underline{A}\) conservative then the value of the line integral depends only on endpoints:
\[I=\phi(-1,0)-\phi(1,0)=-\frac{2}{3}\]
This is clearly a much more elegant solution and may take significantly less working depending on how complex the integral is.

\section{Integral Theorems}
\subsection{Stokes' Theorem}
Stokes's theorem states that for a vector field \(\underline{A}\) that is differentiable everywhere on a surface \(S\), and given that the boundary \(C\) and normal \(\underline{n}\) are both right-oriented. Then:
\[\iint_S(\curl{A})\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S=\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
\subsubsection{Informal Proof}
Recall that:
\[(\curl{A})\cdot\underline{n}=\lim_{\delta S\to 0}\frac{1}{\delta S}\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
Then consider \(N\) finite-sized elements in \(S\):
\[\curl{A}\cdot \underline{n}_i\approx \frac{1}{\delta S_i}\oint_{C_i}\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
\[\sum^N_{i=1}\,\curl{A}\cdot \underline{n}\delta S_i\approx\sum^N_{i=1}\oint_{\delta C_i}\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/stokespf1.png}
    \caption{The surface \(S\) is segmented and curls of each segment calculated.}
\end{figure}
Looking at the LHS of this equation we get:
\[\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum^N_{i=1}\,\curl{A}\cdot\underline{n}\delta S_i=\iint_S(\curl{A})\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S\]
This comes from the definition of an integral. Now consider the RHS of the equation:
\[\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum^N_{i=1}\oint_{\delta C_i}\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
This is because of the right-handed orientation we forced. This means that if we take a close look at two adjacent infinitesimal segments of the surface, their exterior boundary path cancels at the border:
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/stokespf2.png}
    \caption{The bounding paths cancel everywhere except the edges}
\end{figure}
Notice that the only place that this cancellation doesn't happen is around the outside of the surface, since their is no paired path for it to cancel with. Then since we have \(LHS=RHS\) from earlier we can equate our two new expressions and recover Stokes' theorem:
\[\iint_S(\curl{A})\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S=\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}\]
\subsubsection{Non-example}
\begin{align*}
    S:x^2+y^2\leq 1 && C:x^2+y^2=1
\end{align*}
\[\underline{A}=\left(\frac{y}{(x^2+y^2)},\, \frac{-x}{(x^2+y^2)},\, 0\right)\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/stokesnonexample.png}
    \caption{The field is not defined at the origin.}
\end{figure}
Notice that the curl of \(\underline{A}\) is \(0\) via cancelling after applying the quotient rule:
\[\curl{A}=\det{\begin{pmatrix}
    \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\
    \partial x & \partial y & \partial z \\
    \frac{y}{x^2+y^2} & \frac{-x}{x^2+y^2} & 0
\end{pmatrix}}=(0,0,0)=\underline{0}\]
Hence when considering the LHS of stokes' theorem:
\[\iint_S(\curl{A})\cdot\underline{n}\,\text{d}S=0\]
Now lets consider the RHS:
\[\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\int_C\underline{A}(\underline{r})\cdot\df{\underline{r}}{t}\,\text{d}t\]
On \(C\), \(\underline{A}=(y, -x, 0)\). If we parametrise our path then:
\begin{align*}
    x=\cos(t) &&  y=\sin(t) && z=0 && t\in[0,2\pi]
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
    &\underline{r}=(\cos(t),\,\sin(t),\,0) \\
    &\underline{r}'=(-\sin(t),\,\cos(t),\,0) \\
    &\underline{A}(\underline{r})=(\sin(t),\,-\cos(t),\,0)
\end{align*}
\[\oint_C\underline{A}\cdot\underline{\text{d}r}=\int^{2\pi}_0(\sin t,\, -\cos t,\, 0)\cdot (-\sin t,\, \cos t,\, 0)\,\text{d}t=-2\pi\]
Notice that in this case \(LHS\ne RHS\) and stokes' theorem doesn't hold. This is because our condition that \(\underline{A}\) must be differentiable everywhere on \(S\) didn't stand since it was undefined for the point \((0,0,0)\).
\subsection{Green's Theorem}
Set \(\underline{A}=(F(x,y), G(x,y),0)\), \(S\) in the \(x-y\) plane and \(\underline{n}=(0,0,1)\). Then:
\[\iint_SG_x-F_y\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y=\oint_CF\,\text{d}x+G\,\text{d}y\]
\subsection{Constant Vector-Scalar Fields}
Set \(\underline{A}=\underline{a}f\) where \(\underline{a}\) is a constant vector and \(f\) is a scalar field. Then:
\[\iint_S\underline{n}\times \grad{f}\,\text{d}S=\oint_Cf\,\underline{\text{d}r}\]
