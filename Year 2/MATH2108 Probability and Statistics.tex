\section{Bivariate Probability Distributions}
\subsection{Background}
Recall from Year 1, the notion of random variables and the sample space \(\Omega\). Consider the random variable \(X\):
\[X:\Omega\to S\]
If \(S\) is countable then \(X\) is discrete. If \(S\) is uncountable then \(X\) is continuous. Considering the likelihood of each event in the sample space for a discrete random variable, gives the probability mass function. We say that this pmf defines the distribution. We can define the cumulative distribution function to assign meaning to the random variable:
\[F_X:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\]
\[F_X(x)=P[X\leq x],\ x\in\mathbb{R}\]
If \(X\) is continuous, we can further define the probability density function:
\[f_X(x)=\df{F_X(x)}{x}\]
\[\implies F_x(x)=\int^x_{-\infty}f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
Whilst CDFs can be defined for discrete variables, it is important that our outcomes in this case are ordinal. If they are not then there is no meaning to the CDF.
\subsection{Bivariate Distributions}
\subsubsection*{Definitions}
Often, we wish to consider more than one variable at the same time. In these cases, the variables may be correlated. Rather than considering each variable separately, we would like to consider their joint probability distributions. At the beginning of this module we are only going to lok at bivariate probability distributions.
\\
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables. Then the joint CDF of \(X\) and \(Y\) is:
\[F_{X,Y}(x,y)=P[X\leq x,\, Y\leq y]\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}\]
\[F_{X,Y}:\mathbb{R}^2\to [0,1]\]
Suppose \(F_{X,Y}\) is a joint CDF, then if there exists a function \(f_{X,Y}\) such that:
\[F_{X,Y}(x,y)=\int^y_{-\infty}\int^x_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(u,v)\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\]
Then \(f_{X,Y}\) is the joint PDF of \(X\) and \(Y\), with:
\[f_{X,Y}=\pf{^2F_{X,Y}(x,y)}{x\partial y}\]
A bivariate PDF is a surface over the \((x,y)\) plane. This means that calculated probabilities are volumes under the surface, analogous to in 1D where probabilities are areas under the curve.
\subsubsection*{Conditions of existence}
For a joint PDF to be valid it must satisfy the following conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\geq 0\quad \forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\)
    \item \(\int^\infty_{-\infty}\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(u,v)\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v=1\)
    \item \(\forall\,C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2:\)
    \[P[(X,Y)\in C]=\iint_{(x,y)\in C}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Marginal Distributions}
We can also consider \(X\) and \(Y\) from a joint PDF separately. We may only be interested in one of the variables but need to acknowledge its connection to the other. The marginal PDFs of \(X\) and \(Y\) for a bivariate probability distribution are defined as:
\begin{align*}
    &f_X(x)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}y\\
    &f_Y(y)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x
\end{align*}
Likewise:
\begin{align*}
    &F_X(x)=F_{X,Y}(x,\infty)\\
    &F_Y(y)=F_{X,Y}(\infty,y)
\end{align*}
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    e^{-x-y} & x>0,\, y>0\\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Then:
\[f_X(x)=\int^\infty_{0}e^{-x-y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int^t_0e^{-x-y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-e^{-x-y}\right]^t_0\]
\[=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-e^{-x-t}+e^{-x}\right)\]
\[=e^{-x}\]
Hence:
\[f_X(x)=\begin{cases}
    e^{-x} & x>0\\
    0& \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Due to symmetry, the same argument applies for the marginal PDF of \(Y\).
\subsection{Functions of \(X\) and \(Y\)}
Often we will have a function of random variables, for example:
\[Z=g(X,Y)\]
Where \(X\) and \(Y\) have the joint pdf \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\). In this case we may wish to determine the CDF of this function:
\[F_{Z}(z)=P[Z\leq z]=\iint_{\{(x,y):\; g(x.y)\leq z\}}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
Note that this integral is sometimes very difficult or analytically impossible to solve. We will return to functions of random variables later in the module.
\subsection{Independence}
Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be continuous random variables. \(X\) and \(Y\) are said to be independent if:
\[P[X\leq x\cap Y\leq y]=P[X\leq x]\cdot P[Y\leq y]\]
That is:
\[F_{X,Y}(x,y)=F_{X}(x)F_{Y}(y),\quad \forall (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\]
In words this means that having knowledge of the distribution of \(X\), tells us nothing about the distribution of \(Y\). We can formalise this into a somewhat more useful condition:
\[X,Y\text{ are independent}\iff f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)\]
Under some conditions, namely that \(F_{X,Y}\) is differentiable. In this module we do not need to worry about these conditions since all functions we encounter will be suitable "nice". If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then it follows:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=f_X(x)\]
\[f_{Y|X}(y|x)=f_Y(y)\]
\subsubsection*{Example}
Determine if the random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent given their joint pdf:
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    8xy & 0<y<x<1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[f_{X}(x)=\int^x_08xy\,\text{d}y=4x^3\]
\[f_{X}(x)=\begin{cases}
    4x^3 & 0<x<1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[f_{Y}(y)=\int^1_y8xy\,\text{d}x=4y(1-y^2)\]
\[f_{Y}(y)=\begin{cases}
    4y(1-y^2) & 0<y<1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Clearly we have:
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)\ne f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)\]
for some value \((x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) so \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. We could have seen this before calculating however, by just checking the conditions of the joint pdf and noticing a relationship between the values of \(X\) and \(Y\).

\section{Further Bi and Multivariate Probability Distributions}
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent and continuous with pdf's \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\) respectively. We want  to consider \(Z=X+Y\).
\[F_Z(z)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}F_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
It further follows that:
\[f_{X+Y}(z)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\subsection{The Convolution Theorem}
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent and continuous with pdf's \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\) respectively. We want  to consider \(Z=X+Y\).
\[f_{X+Y}(z)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_X(x)f_Y(z-y)\,\text{d}x\]
The function \(f_Z\) is called the convolution of \(f_X\) and \(f_Y\).
\subsection{Functions of Independent Variables}
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables then the functions \(g_1(X)\) and \(g_2(Y)\) are independent for any "nice" functions \(g_1,g_2\). We do not need to define what constitutes a "nice" function in this module.
\subsection{More Than Two Random Variables}
Joint probability distributions may also be known as multivariate probability distributions. Throughout this module we will denote random variables as \(X_1,X_2,...,X_n\). Their joint CDF:
\[F_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=P[X_1\leq x_1,\dots,X_n\leq x_n]\]
Their joint pdf is:
\[f_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\pf{F_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)}{x_1\partial x_2\cdots \partial x_n}\]
Marginals may be defined for subsets as well as single random variables:
\[F_{X_1}(x_1)=F_{X_1,\dots, X_n}(x_1,\infty,\cdots,\infty)\]
\[F_{X_1,X_2,X_4}(x_1,x_2,x_4)=F_{X_1,\dots, X_n}(x_1,x_2,\infty,x_4,\cdots,\infty)\]
Similarly:
\[f_{X_1}(x_1)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots, x_n)\,\text{d}x_2\cdots\text{d}x_n\]
This notation gets quite cumbersome very quickly so we often use vector notation with multivariate distributions. We write \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\) and \(\underline{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\). Then:
\begin{align*}
    &F_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=P[\underline{X}\leq \underline{x}] \\
    &f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=\pf{F_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})}{x_1\partial x_2\cdots \partial x_n} \\
    &P[\underline{X}\in C]=\int_{\underline{x}\in C}f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x}),\quad C\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n
\end{align*}
\subsection{Mutual Independence}
We have mutual independence if:
\[F_{\underline{X}_{(r)}}(\underline{x}_{(r)})=\prod^{|\mathcal{X}_r|}_{j=1}F_{\underline{X}_{(r)_j}}(\underline{x}_{(r)_j})\]
For each subset \(\mathcal{X}_{(r)}\) of \(\{X_1,X_2,\dots\}\). However this kind of sucks, so consider it in the same way as with two variables. We have independence if the joint pdf can be expressed as the product of all of the marginal pdf's. In words, we require that no finite combination of any random variables provides any information about the other variables. Mutual independence is a stronger condition than pairwise independence. The collection of random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are said to be independent and identically distributed if \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are mutually independent.

\section{Conditional Distributions}
\subsection{Conditional PDFs}
Suppose \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) is the joint pdf of the random variables \(X\) and \(Y\). If \(f_Y(y)\) is the marginal pdf of \(Y\) then the conditional pdf of \(X\) given \(Y\) is:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
for \(y\) such that \(f_Y(y)>0\). This implies that:
\[f_X(x)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X|Y}(x|y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
In some cases this form can be easier to calculate than using the joint pdf. Similarly this works for multivariate random variables:
\[f_{X_2,\dots,X_n|X_1}(x_2,\dots,x_n|x_1)=\frac{f_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)}{f_{X_1}(x_1)}\]
\subsection{Calculating Probabilities}
If the random variables \(X\) and \((Y\) are such that \(f_{X|Y}(x|y)\) is the pdf of \(X\) conditional on \(Y\), then for \(C\subseteq\mathbb{R}\):
\[P[X\in C| Y=y]=\int_C f_{X|Y}(x|y)\,\text{d}x\]
\[F_{X|Y}(x|y)=\int^x_{-\infty}f_{X|Y}(u|y)\,\text{d}u\]
Do not overthink this it just the same operation as calculating for a single variable. For the standard pdf of \(X\), we would compute \(P[X<1]\) as:
\[P[X<1]=\int^1_{-\infty}f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
For the conditional \(P[X<1|Y=y]\) we calculate in almost exactly the same way:
\[P[X<1|Y=y]=\int^1_{-\infty}f_{X|Y}(x|y)\,\text{d}x\]
Treat \(y\) as a constant since that is exactly what it is in this context.
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    x+y & 0\leq x,y\leq 1 \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Then:
\[f_Y(y)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\]
\[f_Y(y)=\int^1_0x+y\ \text{d}x\]
\[f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{2}+y&0\leq y\leq 1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
If \(y\notin[0,1]\), then \(f_Y(y)=0\) and so the conditional pdf is not defined. Otherwise:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{x+y}{\frac{1}{2}+y}\]
For \(x,y\in[0,1]\). We can now compute conditional probabilities:
\[P[X\geq \frac{1}{2}|Y=\frac{1}{3}]=\int^1_{\frac{1}{2}}f_{X|Y=\frac{1}{3}}(x|y=\frac{1}{3})\,\text{d}x\]
\[=\int^1_{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{2x+\frac{2}{3}}{\frac{5}{3}}\,\text{d}x\]
\[=0.65\]
\subsection{Independence}
All of the following sentences are equivalent (provided all necessary functions exist):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent
    \item \(f_{X|Y}(x|y)=f_X(x),\ \forall x\in\mathbb{R},\  f_Y(y)>0\)
    \item \(f_{Y|X}(y|x)=f_Y(y),\ \forall y\in\mathbb{R},\  f_X(x)>0\)
\end{enumerate}

\section{Expectation}
\subsection{Definition}
For a single continuous random variable \(X\), with pdf \(f_X(x)\), we know it's expectation:
\[\mathbb{E}(X)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
Moreover:
\[\mathbb{E}(g(X))=\int^\infty_{-\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
We should always be mindful of the distribution to which our expectation relates. We can also consider expectation for the continuous random variables \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\). If \(\underline{X}\) is a multivariate random variable with pdf \(f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})\) and \(g:A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) is a suitable function, then the expectation of \(g(\underline{X})\) is:
\[\mathbb{E}(g(\underline{X}))=\int\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}g(\underline{x})f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})\,\text{d}x\]
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{2}xy & 0\leq x\leq y\leq 2 \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[\mathbb{E}(2X+Y)=\int^2_0\int^y_0(2x+y)(\frac{1}{2}xy)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^2_0\frac{y^4}{3}+\frac{y^4}{4}\,\text{d}y=\frac{56}{15}\]
To help you find the appropriate bounds of integration, consider what happens when you "squish" the function to the axis after integrating over one variable.
\subsection{Linearity of Expectations}
Since expectations are defined via integrals - and integrals are linear - we have:
\[\mathbb{E}(ag_1(\underline{X})+bg_2(\underline{X})+c)=a\mathbb{E}(g_1(\underline{X}))+b\mathbb{E}(g_2(\underline{X}))+c\]
Where \(g_1,g_2:A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}\). Additionally, for any collection of random variables:
\[\mathbb{E}\left(\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\right)=\sum^n_{i=1}\mathbb{E}(X_i)\]
Note that this property holds without any conditions on independence.
\subsection{Expectations of Functions}
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables, then for any functions \(g,h\):
\[\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[(g(X)]\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\]
This comes from the integral definition, and the key property of independence.
\[\int^\infty_{-\infty}\int^\infty_{-\infty}g(x)h(y)f_X(x)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\int^\infty_{-\infty}h(y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\subsection{Covariance and Correlation}
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables. The covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\) is:
\[\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]\]
This is clearly \(0\) if \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent but the converse is not true.
\subsubsection{Properties of covariance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\text{Cov}(X,X)=\text{Var}(X)\)
    \item \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\text{Cov}(Y,X)\)
    \item \(\text{Cov}(aX+b,\,cY+d)=ac\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\)
\end{itemize}
For random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_m\) and \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) we have:
\[\text{Cov}\left[\left(\sum^m_{i=1}a_iX_i\right),\,\left(\sum^n_{i=1}b_jY_j\right)\right]=\sum^m_{i=1}\sum^n_{j=1}a_ib_j\,\text{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)\]
Using this it is not too hard to see that:
\[\text{Var}\left(\sum^n_{i=1}a_iX_i\right)=\underbrace{\sum^n_{i=1}a_i^2\,\text{Var}(X_i)}_{i=j}+2\underbrace{\sum_{1\leq i< j\leq n}a_ia_j\,\text{Cov}(X_i,X_j)}_{i\ne j}\]
Where the two comes from the symmetry of the variance-covariance matrix, which in turn exists because of commutativity. This means that without loss of generality we can set \(i<j\).
\subsubsection*{Example}
\begin{align*}
    &\mathbb{E}[X]=1 && \text{Var}[X]=2 \\
    &\mathbb{E}[Y]=2 && \text{Var}[Y]=4 \\
    &\text{Cov}(X,Y)= 1
\end{align*}
We wish to compute Cov\((2X+Y,\,2Y-X)\):
\[\text{Cov}\left(\sum^m_{i=1}a_iX_i,\, \sum^n_{j=1}b_jY_j\right)=\sum^m_{i=1}\sum^n_{j=1}a_ib_j\, \text{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)\]
\begin{align*}
    \text{Cov}(2X+Y,\,2Y-X)=&\\ 
    &\underbrace{(2\cdot2)\text{Cov}(X,Y)}_{2X\cdot2Y} \\
    +&\underbrace{(1\cdot2)\text{Var}(Y)}_{Y\cdot 2Y} \\
    -&(1\cdot2)\text{Var}(X) \\
    -&(1\cdot1)\text{Cov}(X,Y)\\
    =&3\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)+2\,\text{Var}(Y)-2\,\text{Var}(X)=7
\end{align*}
\subsection{Variance-Covariance Matrix}
For the multivariate random variable \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\), the variance-covariance matrix of \(\underline{X}\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix whose \((r,s)\) entry is given by \(\text{Cov}(X_r,X_s)\). It is denoted \(\Sigma\).
\[\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}
    \text{Var}(X_1) & \text{Cov}(X_1,X_2) & \cdots & \text{Cov}(X_1,X_n) \\
    \text{Cov}(X_2,X_1) & \text{Var} (X_2) & \cdots & \text{Cov}(X_2,X_n) \\
    \vdots & \cdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    \text{Cov}(X_n,X_1) & \text{Cov}(X_n, X_2) & \cdots & \text{Var}(X_n)
\end{pmatrix}\]
Note that in order to calculate the variance-covariance matrix, we need the following information:
\begin{align*}
    &\mathbb{E}[X] && \mathbb{E}[X^2] \\
    &\mathbb{E}[Y] && \mathbb{E}[Y^2] \\
    &\mathbb{E}[XY]
\end{align*}
\subsubsection{An important lemma}
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables such that \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0\) and \(\text{Var}(X)=\text{Var}(Y)=1\) then:
\[|\mathbb{E}[XY]|\leq 1\]
You might be wondering when we have those conditions for this lemma to be true, and it turns out this is always the case after we have standardised the random variables. This is a useful operation we will do a lot so this lemma comes into effect more times than you would think.
\subsection{Cauchy-Schwartz Inequality}
For any pair of random variables \(X\) and \(Y\):
\[|\text{Cov}(X,Y)|^2\leq \text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)\]
The real power of this statement is in the word "any".
\subsection{Correlation}
Sometimes we prefer to use a measure that is independent of the magnitude of the data. One such measure is the Pearson correlation:
\[\rho(X,Y)=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)}}\]
This is dimensionless and provides a measure of linear association between \(X\) and \(Y\).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(|\rho|\leq 1\)
    \item If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then \(\rho(X,Y)=0\) but the converse is not true.
    \item For constants \(a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}\):
    \[\rho(aX+b,\, cY+d)=\begin{cases}
        \rho(X,Y)& ac>0 \\
        -\rho(X,Y)&ac<0
    \end{cases}\]
\end{itemize}

\section{Conditional Expectation and Variance}
\subsection{Conditional Expectation}
Recall that the conditional pdf of random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) is given by:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
subject to \(f_Y(y)>0\). Now suppose that \(X\) and \(Y\) are random variables. The conditional expectation of \(X\) given \(Y=y\) is:
\[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x|y)\,\text{d}x\]
Notice that this is a one dimensional integral since we are conditioning on \(Y=y\) being constant. We can also assume \(Y\) remains a random variable by substituting \(y\) for \(Y\) in our expectation.
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{4x}{y} & 0\leq x< y \leq 1 \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
We wish to calculate \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]\).
\begin{align*}
    f_Y(y)&=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{4x}{y}\,\text{d}x \\\\
    &=\int^y_0\frac{4x}{y}\,\text{d}x \\\\
    &=\left[\frac{2x^2}{y}\right]^y_0 \\\\
    &=\begin{cases}
        2y & 0\leq y \leq 1 \\
        0&\text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}
\[f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
When \(0<y\leq 1\) this is defined and we have:
\[f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{2x}{y^2} & 0<x<y \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Finally we have:
\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x\mid y)\,\text{d}x\]
\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=\frac{2}{3}y\]
If we were instead looking for \(Y\) to remain a random variable then:
\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\frac{2}{3}Y\].
\subsubsection{Functions of \(X\)}
We can also defined conditional random variables such as \(\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y]\) and indeed for any suitable function \(f(X)\) we can define \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]\). This naturally leads us to define conditional variances:
\[\text{Var}[X|Y=y]=\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y=y]-(\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y])^2\]
\subsection{The Tower Property}
Because \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y]\) is in itself a random variable, we can take the expectation of it. If we do this, our expectation will be considering the marginal of \(Y\). When dealing with lots of these expectations, it may make sense to use the older notation \(\mathbb{E}_Y\) to denote which pdf our expectation is taken with respect to. For any pair of random variables \(X\) and \(Y\):
\[\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]\]
Note that this holds for both discrete and continuous random variables and even if \(X\) and \(Y\) are not the same type. This is a very important result that gets used all the time.
\subsubsection{Proof of the tower property}
Consider \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y]\):
\[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x\mid y)\,\text{d}x = g(Y)\]
This is a random variable and a function of \(Y\).
\[\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]\]
\[\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]=\int^\infty_{\infty}g(y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\left\{\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x\mid y)\,\text{d}x\right\}\,\text{d}y\]
Note that:
\[f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
\[\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}x\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}y\ \text{d}x\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
\[=\mathbb{E}[X]\]
\subsubsection{Continuous case example}
Suppose \(Y\sim\text{Gamma}(2,\theta)\) and \(X|Y=y\sim\text{Exp}(y)\). Then:
\[\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]=\frac{1}{y}\]
\[\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{y}\right]\]
\[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{Y}\right]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{1}{y}f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\] 
\[=\int^\infty_0\frac{1}{y}\theta^2ye^{-\theta y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\theta\int^\infty_0\theta e^{-\theta y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\theta\]
Since the remaining integral is a pdf and so integrates to \(1\) over its entire domain.
\subsection{Conditional Probabilities of Events}
If \(X\) is a random variable and \(A\) is some event of interest then:
\[\mathbb{P}[A]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\mathbb{P}[A|X=x]f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
This is analogous to the law of total probabilities in the discrete world. In particular we can consider the random variable:
\[Y=\begin{cases}
    1 & \text{If event A occurs} \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
An extension of this, and another way of proving an old result, is that if \(X\) and \(Y\) are random variables, then:
\[f_Y(y)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
\subsection{Wald's Equation}
Suppose \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) are a sequence of i.i.d random variables and \(N\) is some non-negative integer valued random variable, independent of the sequence. Then if:
\[T=\sum^N_{i=1}X_i\]
We have:
\[\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[N]\mathbb{E}[X_1]\]
This comes from applying the tower property and independence. Note that if \(N=0\) we define \(T=0\).
\subsubsection*{Example}
Suppose a hen lays \(N\) eggs a week and \(X_i\) is the weight of the \(i^{th}\) egg. We want to know the average total weight of all eggs in one week.
\[N\sim \text{Po}(\lambda)\]
\[X_i\sim N(\mu,\,\sigma^2)\]
\[T=\sum^N_{i=1}X_i\]
\[\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[N]\mathbb{E}[X_1]=\lambda\mu\]
Notice that this makes sense physically. This statement says that the average total weight of eggs per week is the average amount of eggs multiplied by the average weight of one egg.
\subsection{More Properties of Expectations}
For constants \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\) and random variables \(X,Y,Z\):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\mathbb{E}[aX+bY|Z]=a\mathbb{E}[X|Z]+b\mathbb{E}[Y|Z]\)
    \item If \(Y\geq 0\) then \(\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\geq 0\)
    \item For \(a\in\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbb{E}[a|X]=a\)
    \item If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]\)
    \item For any 'nice' \(g\): \(\mathbb{E}[Xg(Y)|Y]=g(Y)\mathbb{E}[X|Y]\)
    \item For random variables \(X,Y,Z\):
    \[\mathbb{E}[X|Z]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y,Z]\mid Z]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Z]\mid Y,Z]\]
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Conditional Variance}
For the random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) we have already seen that we can construct the conditional variance of \(X|Y\):
\[\text{Var}[X|Y]=\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y]-(\mathbb{E}[X|Y])^2\]
For any random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) where \(\text{Var}[X]\) is defined:
\[\text{Var}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}[X|Y]]+\text{Var}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]\]
This equation is incredibly useful.
\subsubsection{Proof}
\[\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}[X|Y]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y]-\mathbb{E}[X|Y]^2]\]
Via the tower property, this simplifies:
\[\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}[X|Y]]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]^2]\]
Now consider:
\[\text{Var}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]^2]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y]]\]
Which again simplifies, via the tower property:
\[\text{Var}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]^2]-\mathbb{E}[X^2]\]
Then we have the statement:
\[\text{Var}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]^2]+\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2\]
\[\text{Var}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2\]
Which means we have recovered the definition we know and love.
\[\blacksquare\]

\section{Probability Generating Functions}
\subsection{Motivation}
Generating functions help us extract or compute helpful properties and statistics of random variables. In MATH1102, probability generating functions were covered for discrete random variables. In this module we look at the more general moment generating functions with an emphasis on continuous variables.
\subsection{Moment Generating Functions}
For any random variable \(X\), the moment generating function (MGF) of \(X\) is the function:
\[M_X:A\subseteq \mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)\]
with:
\[M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]\]
The MGF is defined as long as this expectation exists and is finite in an open interval around \(t=0\).
\subsubsection{Computing a MGF}
Let \(X\sim N(0,1)\). We wish to determine the MGF of \(X\).
\[f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\]
\[M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]\]
\[M_X(t)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}e^{tx}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)\,\text{d}x\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}+tx\right)\,\text{d}x\]
\[=e^{\frac{t^2}{2}}\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-t)^2\right)\,\text{d}x\]
Notice that we have intentionally kept the constant inside the integral because now we have it in a form we can easily compute. This is because the integrand is the pdf of the distribution \(N(t,1)\) and as such since we are integrating over it's entire domain, we are just left with \(1\). Thus:
\[M_X(t)=e^{\frac{t^2}{2}}\]
\subsubsection{MGF with restricted domain}
Consider \(Y\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\). The MGF of \(Y\) is then given by:
\[M_Y(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]=\int^\infty_0e^{ty}\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\lambda\int^\infty_0e^{y(t-\lambda)}\,\text{d}y\]
But notice that this integral will not be finite if we do not have \(t<\lambda\). Under this condition:
\[t\in(-\infty, \lambda)\]
We also know from the pdf of \(Y\) that:
\[\lambda > 0\]
Luckily in this case, this guarantees that we have an open interval around \(t=0\) for which the integral is finite and so the MGF exists. It is possible that either our conditions on \(t\), or our integrand do not satisfy this open-interval requirement and hence the MGF does not exist.
\subsection{Properties of MGFs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(M_X(0)=1\), for any random variable \(X\).
    \item We can take derivatives of our MGF, subject to the definition:
    \[M_X^{(n)}(t)=\pf{^nM_X(t)}{t^n}\]
    and in this case:
    \[M_X^{(n)}(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^ne^{tX}]\]
    Notice that when \(t=0\), we recover the \(n^{th}\) moment of \(X\).
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Taylor Expansions of MGFs}
Recall that the Maclaurin expansion of \(f(t)=e^{tx}\) is given by:
\[e^{tx}=\sum^\infty_{n=0}\frac{x^n}{n!}t^n\]
and then:
\[M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\left(\sum^\infty_{n=0}\frac{x^n}{n!}t^n\right)\, f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
\[=\sum^\infty_{n=0}\frac{t^n}{n!}\int^\infty_{-\infty}x^nf_X(x)\,\text{d}x=\sum^\infty_{n=0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^n]}{n!}t^n\]
This strategy can be very useful to find the moments of \(X\), or maybe find a general formula for them.
\subsubsection*{Example}
Let \(M_Y(t)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}\). Then:
\[M_Y(t)=n!\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-(n+1)}\]
\[M_Y(t)=\sum^\infty_{n=0}\frac{M_Y^{(n)}(0)}{n!}t^n=\sum^\infty_{n=0}\frac{1}{\lambda^n}t^n\]
Equating coefficients allows us to see:
\[\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^n]}{n!}=\frac{1}{\lambda^n}\implies \mathbb{E}[X^n]=\frac{n!}{\lambda^n}\]
\subsection{Uniqueness of MGFs}
Suppose that \(X\) and\(Y\) are random variables with MGFs \(M_X(t)\) and \(M_Y(t)\) respectively. Then \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same distribution if and only if \(M_X(t)=M_Y(t)\) for all \(t\) in an open interval containing 0. Note that this theorem does not mean that random variables with the same moments have the same distribution.
\subsection{Linear Transformations}
If a random variable \(X\) has MGF \(M_X(t)\) then the MGF of \(Y=a+bX\) is:
\[M_Y(t)=e^{at}M_X(bt)\]
\subsection{Joint Probability Distributions: MGFs}
Suppose \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^\mathrm{T}\) is a multivariate random variable with joint pdf \(f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})\). Define \(\underline{t}=(t_1,\dots,t_n)^\mathrm{T}\), then:
\[M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})=\mathbb{E}[e^{\underline{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{x}}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{t_1X_1+\cdots+t_nX_n}]\]
as long as \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\underline{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{x}}]\) exists and is defined in an open region around:
\[t_1=\cdots = t_n=0\]
\subsubsection{Properties of joint MGFs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})=M_{\underline{Y}}(\underline{t})\iff F_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=F_{\underline{Y}}(\underline{y})\)
    \item The marginal MGF of \(X_i\) can be recovered by setting all \(t_j=0\) where \(j\ne i\):
    \[M_{X_i}(0,\dots,t,\,\dots,0)=\mathbb{E}[e^{t_iX_i}]\]
    \item We can find moments by taking partial derivatives and evaluating at \(\underline{t}=0\):
    \[\pf{^{(n)}M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})}{t_i}\bigr|_{\underline{t}=0}=\mathbb{E}[X_i^n]\]
    \item The random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are mutually independent if and only if:
    \[M_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(\underline{t})=\prod^n_{i=1}M_{X_i}(t_i)\]
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Sums of Independent Random Variables}
If \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) are mutually independent random variables and \(Y\) is defined such that:
\[Y=\sum^n_{i=1}c_iX_i\]
Where \(c_1,\dots,c_n\in\mathbb{R}\) are constants. Then the MGF of \(Y\) is:
\[M_Y(t)=\prod^n_{i=1}M_{X_i}(c_it_i)\]
A special case is when \(c_1=\cdots=c_n=1\).
\subsubsection{Proof of sums of normal i.i.d variables}
Suppose that \(X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma^2_i)\) for \(i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\) with \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) mutually independent. Then let:
\[Y=\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\]
and recall:
\[M_Y(t)=\prod^n_{i=1}M_{X_i}(t)\]
We can find via integration that:
\[M_{X_i}(t)=\exp\left(\mu_i t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2t^2\right)\]
Then:
\[\prod^n_{i=1}M_{X_i}(t)=\prod^n_{i=1}\exp\left(\mu_it+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2t^2\right)\]
\[=\exp\left(\mu_1t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_1^2t^2+\cdots+\mu_nt+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_n^2t^2\right)\]
\[=\exp\left((\mu_1+\cdots\mu_n)t+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_1^2+\cdots\sigma^2_n)t^2\right)\]
Let:
\begin{align*}
    \mu^*=\sum^n_{i=1}\mu_i&&(\sigma^{2})^*=\sum^\infty_{i=1}\sigma^2_i
\end{align*}
Then:
\[M_Y(t)=\exp\left(\mu^*t+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^2)^*t^2\right)\]
Which, by the uniqueness of MGFs, is the MGF for a normal distribution. Hence:
\[Y=\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\sim N(\mu_1+\cdots+\mu_n,\, \sigma^2_1+\cdots\sigma^2_n)\]
Note that we can extend this proof rather simply to show the central limit theorem.
\subsubsection{Sums of \(\chi^2\) random variables}
Suppose the random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are mutually independent with \(X_i\sim\chi^2_{\nu_i}\) for \(i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\). Note that \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are not iid since the degrees of freedom vary between each variable. We have that:
\[M_{X_i}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-2t)^{\frac{\nu_i}{2}}},\quad t<\frac{1}{2}\]
Consider \(S=\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\):
\[M_S(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tS}]\]
\[=\mathbb{E}[e^{t(X_1+\cdots+X_n)}]\]
\[=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_1}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_2}]\cdots\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_n}]\]
\[=\prod^n_{i=1}\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_i}]\]
\[=\prod^n_{i=1}M_{X_i}(t)\]
Therefore:
\[M_S(t)=\prod^n_{i=1}\frac{1}{(1-2t)^{\frac{\nu_i}{2}}},\quad t<\frac{1}{2}\]
\[=(1-2t)^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum^n_{i=1}\nu_i}\]
Notice that this has the functional form of a MGF of a \(\chi^2\) distribution. Since MGFs uniquely defined distributions, we have:
\[S\sim\chi^2_r\]
Where \(r=\sum^n_{i=1}\nu_i\). This is a very important result that we will use extensively in statistics.
\subsection{Probability Generating Functions Recap}
Recall that for a random variable \(X\) that takes values in a subset of \(\{0,1,2,\dots\}\) with probability mass function \(p_X(x)\): The PGF of \(X\), \(\phi_X:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) is defined as:
\[\phi_X(s)=\mathbb{E}[s^X]=\sum^\infty_{x=0}s^xp_X(x)\]
PGFs have some nice properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(p_X(t)=p_Y(t)\iff\phi_X(s)=\phi_Y(s)\)
    \item \(\phi_X(1)=1\)
    \item \(\phi^{(n)}_X(1)=\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)\cdots(X-(n-1))]\)
    \item \(\phi^{(n)}_X(0)=n!P[X=x]\)
    \item If \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are independent then \(Y=\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\) has PGF:
    \[\phi_Y(s)=\prod^n_{i=1}\phi_{X_i}(s)\]
\end{itemize}
Notice the following relationship between PGFs and MGFs:
\[M_X(t)=\phi_X(e^t)\iff M_X(\log s)=\phi_X(s)\]
Where both the MGF are PGF are suitably defined.

\section{Inequalities and Limit Theorems}
\subsection{Motivation}
Sometimes we cannot compute probabilities exactly. In cases like this, we may still be interested in placing a bound on the probability of an event.
\subsection{Markov's Inequality}
Suppose \(X\geq 0\) is a random variable and \(c>0\) is a constant, then:
\[P[X\geq c]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{c}\]
\subsubsection{Proof}
Suppose \(X\geq 0\) is a random variable and \(c>0\) is a constant. Let \(Y\) be a random variable defined by:
\[Y=\begin{cases}
    1 & X\geq c \\
    0 & X < c
\end{cases}\]
Then we have \(Y\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)\), where \(p=P[Y=1]=P[X\geq c]\). Consider \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\):
\[\mathbb{E}[Y]=\sum_yyP[Y=y]=P[Y=1]=P[X\geq c]\]
\[X\geq c\implies X\geq cY\]
\[\implies \mathbb{E}[X]\geq \mathbb{E}[cY]\]
\[\implies\mathbb{E}[X]\geq c\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y]\]
\[\implies \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{c}\geq P[X\geq c]\]
\subsubsection{Example}
Suppose \(X\) is a random variable representing the annual income of a worker in the UK. We wish to determine an upper bound for the proportion of the population that earns more than 3 times the mean annual income. In symbols, we wish to calculate:
\[P[X\geq 3\mathbb{E}[X]]\]
\(X\geq0\) and so \(\mathbb{E}[X]>0\), hence we can use Markov's inequality:
\[P[X\geq 3\mathbb{E}[X]]\leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{3\mathbb{E}[X]}=\frac{1}{3}\]
So less than a third of the population earn more than 3 times the mean annual income. A quick sanity check tells us that makes sense, especially since our choice of average as the mean.
\subsection{Chebyshev's Inequality}
If \(X\) is a random variable with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(c\) is a constant, then:
\[P[|X-\mu|\geq c]\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}\]
What this says in words is that we can put a bound on the probability we will be some given distance away from the mean given some information about the distribution of our random variable. Unlike Markov's, Chebyshev's holds for negative \(X\).
\subsubsection{Proof}
Let \(X\) be a random variable with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(c\) is a constant, then notice that:
\[P[|X-\mu|\geq c]=P[(X-\mu)^2\geq c^2]\]
Since \((X-\mu)^2\geq 0\), we can apply Markov's inequality to this:
\[P[(X-\mu)^2\geq c^2]\leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{c^2}\]
A little bit of cleaning up gets us directly to Chebyshev's inequality:
\[P[|X-\mu|\geq c]\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}\]
\subsubsection{Example and comparison to Markov's}
Suppose we have the random variable \(X\sim\text{Exp}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\) and we wish to calculate \(P[X\geq 10]\). We can directly apply Markov's inequality to get:
\[P[X\geq 10]\leq \frac{2}{10}=\frac{1}{5}\]
We can then apply Chebyshev's inequality to see:
\[P[|X-2|\geq c]\leq \frac{4}{c^2}\]
We can find the appropriate \(c\) by expanding the absolute value:
\[P[\{x-2\leq -c\}\cup\{x-2\geq 2\}]\]
Choose \(c=8\):
\[P[\{X\leq-6\}\cup\{X\geq 10\}]\]
Apply properties of the union and notice that since \(X\) takes an exponential distribution it cannot have a negative value:
\[P[|X-2|\geq 8]=P[X\geq 10]\]
\[P[X\geq 10]\leq \frac{4}{64}=\frac{1}{16}\]
Notice that Chebyshev's inequality has given us a tighter bound than Markov's gave - this is due to it requiring more information about the distribution of \(X\) to calculate.
\subsection{Weak Law of Large Numbers}
Suppose \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid with \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\mu\) and \(\text{Var}[X_i]=\sigma^2<\infty\). Then for any \(\varepsilon>0\):
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left[\left|\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}-\mu\right|>\varepsilon\right]=0\]
The weak law of large numbers if often described in words as the property "converges in probability". In words essentially the law says that the sample mean of a set of iid random variables converges to the true mean of one variable in that we can get arbitrarily close for a large enough sample size. We would say \(\bar{X}\) converges in probability to \(\mu\). We may write this as:
\[\bar{X}\overset{p}{\to}\mu,\quad n\to\infty\]
\subsection{Strong Law of Large Numbers}
Suppose that \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid with \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\mu\) and \(\text{Var}[X_i]=\sigma^2<\infty\). Then:
\[P\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}=\mu\right]=1\]
Notice that the strong law of large numbers implies the weak law but not the other way around. We describe the strong law of large numbers in words as the property 'almost sure convergence'. The law says that as we increase our sample size to infinity, the sample mean will exactly equal the true mean of one variable. We may write this as:
\[\bar{X}\overset{a.s}{\to}\mu,\quad n\to\infty\]
Neither of these laws tell us anything about the variability of \(\bar{X}\). To comment on this we need to use the property known as convergence in distribution.
\subsection{Convergence in Distribution}
Suppose \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is a sequence of random variables with CDFs: \(F_1,\dots,F_n\) and \(X\) is a random variable with CDF \(F\). Then we say \(X_n\) converges in distribution to \(X\) as \(n\to\infty\) if:
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}F_n(x)=F(x)\]
Alternatively:
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{X_n}(t)=M_X(t)\]
We often write this property as:
\[X_n\overset{D}{\to}X,\quad n\to\infty\]
\subsubsection{Example: binomial to Poisson}
Suppose \(X_n\sim\text{Bin}(n,\,\frac{\lambda}{n})\). Now let \(Y\sim\text{Bin}(n,\,p)\).
\[M_Y(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]\]
\[=\sum^n_{y=0}e^{ty}P[Y=y]\]
\[=\sum^n_{y=0}\binom{n}{y}(pe^t)^y(1-p)^{n-y}\]
\[=(pe^t+1-p)^n\]
Then:
\[M_{X_n}(t)=\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{n}e^t-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^n\]
\[=\left(1+\frac{\lambda(e^t-1)}{n}\right)^n\]
As \(n\to\infty\) we have \(M_{X_n}(t)\to\exp(\lambda(e^t-1))\). However this is the functional form of a Poisson MGF with mean \(\lambda\). Thus:
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{X_n}(t)=M_X(t)\]
So \(X_n\overset{D}{\to}X\) as \(n\to\infty\).
\subsection{Central Limit Theorem}
Suppose \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid with finite mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). If:
\[S_n=\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\]
and:
\[Z_n=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\]
We have:
\[Z_n\overset{D}{\to}Z\sim N(0,1)\]
This is a hugely important result and notice how little restrictions there are on the random variables themselves. This makes the theorem widely applicable.

\section{Multivariate Normal Distribution}
\subsection{Introduction and Notation}
The multivariate normal distribution is a multidimensional generalisation of the the univariate normal distribution. Suppose \(\Sigma\) is an \(n\times n\) real valued matrix. Then \(\Sigma\) is called positive definite if for all \(\underline{X}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(\underline{X}\ne 0\)::
\[\underline{X}^T\Sigma \underline{X}>0\]
An affine transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) takes the form:
\[\underline{X}\mapsto A\underline{X} +\underline{b}\]
Where:
\begin{align*}
    A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} && \underline{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m
\end{align*}
The rank of \(A\) is the maximum number of linearly independent columns.
\subsection{Definition}
The multivariate random variable \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\) has an \(n\)-dimensional multivariate normal distribution if:
\[\underline{X}=\underline{\mu}+A\underline{Z}\]
We have \(\underline{\mu}=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n)^T\) is a column vector of means. \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) has rank \(k\leq n\) and we have \(\underline{Z}=(Z_1,\dots,Z_n)^T\) as a vector of independent standard normal random variables. Notice:
\[\mathbb{E}[\underline{X}]=\mathbb{E}[\underline{\mu}+A\underline{Z}]=\underline{\mu}+A\mathbb{E[\underline{Z}]}=\underline{\mu}\]
\[\text{Var}[\underline{X}]=A\text{Var}[\underline{Z}]A^T=AA^T\]
Set \(\Sigma=AA^T\) then we write:
\[\underline{X}\sim \mathcal{N}_n(\underline{\mu},\Sigma)\]
\subsection{Probability Density Function}
If \(\Sigma\) is full rank then it is positive definite and invertible. Therefore the pdf of \(\underline{X}\) is:
\[f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}|\Sigma|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\underline{x}-\underline{\mu})^T\Sigma^{-1}(\underline{x}-\underline{\mu})\right)\]
If \(\Sigma\) is not full rank then \(\Sigma^{-1}\) doesn't exist and so the pdf takes a much more complex form.
\subsection{MGF}
Because \(\underline{Z}=(Z_1,\dots,Z_n)^T\) is a collection of independent random variables we have:
\[M_{\underline{Z}}(\underline{t})=\prod^n_{i=1}M_{Z_i}(t_i)\]
Additionally we know:
\[\underline{Z}\sim \mathcal{N}_n(\underline{0}, I_n)\]
\[M_{\underline{Z}}(\underline{t})=\exp \left(\frac{1}{2}\sum^n_{i=1}t_i^2\right)\]
Consider \(\underline{X}=\underline{\mu}+A\underline{Z}\). Then:
\[M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\underline{t}^T(\underline{\mu}+A\underline{Z}))]=e^{\underline{t}^T\underline{\mu}}M_{\underline{Z}}(A^T\underline{t})\]
\[M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})=\exp\left(\underline{t}^T\underline{\mu}+\frac{1}2{\underline{t}^T\Sigma\underline{t}}\right)\]
This is the MGF of \(\underline{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\underline{\mu},\Sigma)\).
\subsubsection{Using the MGF to find moments}
It can be helpful to rewrite the MGF in component form:
\[M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})=\exp\left(\sum^n_{i=1}t_i\mu_i+\frac{1}{2}\sum^n_{i=1}\sum^n_{j=1}t_i\sigma_{ij}t_j\right)\]
Where \(\sigma_{ij}\) are the \((i,j)^{th}\) element of \(\Sigma\).
\[\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}
    \sigma_{11}&\cdots&\sigma_{1n} \\
    \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    \sigma_{n1} & \cdots & \sigma_{nn}
\end{pmatrix}\]
We need to differentiate \(M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})\) with respect to \(t_r\), \(r\in\{1,\dots,n\}\).
\[\pf{}{t_r}M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})=(\mu_r+\sum^n_{i=1}t_i\sigma_{ir})M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})\]
\[\mathbb{E}[X_r]=\mu_rM_{\underline{X}}(\underline{0})=\mu_r\]
\[\mathbb{E}[X_rX_s]=\pf{^2}{t_st_r}M_{\underline{X}}(\underline{t})\bigr|_{t=0}=\sigma_{sr}+\mu_s\mu_r\]
Then \(\text{Cov}(X_s,X_r)=\sigma_{sr}\).
\subsection{Bivariate Normal Distribution}
When \(n\) is small we can write out the variance-covariance matrix and pdf explicitly. When \(n=2\) we hae a bivariate normal distribution:
\[\underline{X}\sim \mathcal{N}_2(\underline{\mu},\Sigma)\]
Where:
\begin{align*}
    \underline{X}=\begin{pmatrix}
        X_1 \\
        X_2
    \end{pmatrix} && \underline{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}
        \mu_1 \\
        \mu_2
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
\[\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}
    \sigma_1^2 && \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\
    \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 && \sigma_2^2
\end{pmatrix}\]
\(\Sigma\) is non-singular if and only if \(|\rho|<1\) which makes sense since \(\rho=1\) implies that the variables have no independence. Note that if \(\rho=0\) then:
\[f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=f_{X_1}(x_1)f_{X_2}(x_2)\]
\begin{align*}
    X_1\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) && X_2\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)
\end{align*}
Notice this does not hold generally.
\subsubsection{Properties}
Suppose \(X\sim \mathcal{N}_n(\underline{\mu},\Sigma)\) and \(\underline{Y}=C\underline{X}+\underline{b}\) with:
\begin{align*}
    \underline{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m && C\in\mathbb{\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}  
\end{align*}
Then:
\[\underline{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}_m(C\underline{\mu}+\underline{b},\  C\Sigma C^T)\]
If you want to calculate marginals, you can take an affine transformation with \(\underline{b}=0\) and \(C\) a \(1\times n\) matrix with \(1\) in column \(r\) and \(0\) elsewhere. Then:
\[Y=C\underline{X}+\underline{b}=X_r\]
\subsubsection*{Example}
Suppose \(\underline{X}=(X_1,X_2,X_3)^T\), \(\underline{\mu}=(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3)^T\) and \(\Sigma\) be the positive definite matrix:
\[\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}
    \sigma_1^2 & \gamma_{12} & \gamma_{13} \\
    \gamma_{21} & \sigma_{2}^2 & \gamma_{23} \\
    \gamma_{31} & \gamma_{32} & \sigma_{3}^2
\end{pmatrix}\]
We want to find the distribution of \((X_2,X_3)^T\). Let \(\underline{Y}=C\underline{X}\) where:
\[C=\begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}\]
\(\underline{Y}\) is an affine transformation of a multivariate normal distribution. Hence \(\underline{Y}\) has a bivariate normal distribution.
\[\underline{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}_2(\mathbb{E}[\underline{Y}],\, \text{Var}[\underline{Y}])\]
\[\mathbb{E}[\underline{Y}]=C\mathbb{E}[\underline{X}]=\begin{pmatrix}
    X_2 \\
    X_3
\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\text{Var}[\underline{Y}]=C\text{Var}[\underline{X}]C^T=\begin{pmatrix}
    \sigma_2^2 & \sigma_{23} \\
    \sigma_{32} & \sigma_3^2
\end{pmatrix}\]
\subsection{Independence}
Suppose \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\) are such that \(\underline{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\underline{\mu}, \Sigma)\). Then the components \(X_1,\dots ,X_n\) are independent if and only if they are uncorrelated.
\subsection{Conditional distributions}
We can compute multivariate conditional distributions. We do this with exactly the same method as normal, using the joint and marginal distributions. In the bivariate normal case:
\[X_2|X_1=x_1\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2+\rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x_1-\mu_1),\  \sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2))\]
\subsection{Degenerative case}
So far we have only considered cases where \(\Sigma\) is full rank. If \(\Sigma\) is not full rank then whilst we still have:
\[\underline{X}=\underline{\mu}+A\underline{Z}\]
However, whilst \(\underline{X}\) appears to take values in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), it actually only exists in \(\mathbb{R}^k,\ k<n\) because we can always construct the other variables using the first \(k\). This reduces the dimensions of the dataset / problem.

\section{Transformations of Random Variables}
\subsection{One-dimensional Transformations}
Suppose \(X\) is a continuous random variable with known pdf. We define the random variable:
\[Y=g(X)\]
for some function \(g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\). If we wish to know the pdf of \(Y\) we might take the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Determine the possible values/support for \(Y\).
    \item For each \(y\), consider \(F_Y(y)\).
    \item Note that the event \(\{Y\leq y\}\) is equivalent \(\{X\leq g^{-1}(y)\}\).
    \item Deduce \(F_Y(y)=F_X(g^{-1}(y))\).
    \item Differentiate to get \(f_Y(y)\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection{Example}
Suppose \(X\) is a random variable such that \(X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) with \(\lambda >0\). We wish to find the pdf of \(Y=cX\) where \(c>0\) is a constant. First realise that \(Y\) can take any positive value and note that \(f_Y(y)=0\) when \(y\leq 0\). If \(y>0\):
\[F_Y(y)=P[Y\leq y]=P[cX\leq y]=P\left[X\leq \frac{y}{c}\right]\]
\[P\left[X\leq \frac{y}{c}\right]=\int^{\frac{y}{c}}_0f_X(x)\,\text{d}x=1-e^{-\frac{\lambda y}{c}}\]
\[f_Y(y)=F'_Y(y)=\frac{\lambda}{c}e^{-\frac{\lambda y}{c}}\]
\[f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{\lambda}{c}e^{-\frac{\lambda y}{c}}& y>0 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
This means that \(Y\sim \text{Exp}\left(\frac{\lambda}{c}\right)\).
\subsection{Monotone Transformations}
It should be clear that we can only follow a method like this when our function has a well defined inverse. This is certainly not the case all the time, however it is true when we have a strictly monotone function. In higher dimensions we require injectivity to ensure that our function has an inverse. Suppose \(X\) is a continuous random variable with support \(A\) and \(g:A\to\mathbb{R}\) is a strictly monotonic function that is differentiable on \(A\). Then the pdf of \(Y=g(X)\) is:
\[f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
    f_X(g^{-1}(y))\left|\pf{}{y}g^{-1}(y)\right| & y\in g(A) \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\subsubsection{The standard normal}
We can use the above formula to prove that a standard transformation results in a normal distribution. Suppose \(X\sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) and \(Y=g(X)=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\). Then it shouldn't be hard to see that \(g(X)\) is strictly increasing and \(A=g(A)\) is the support of \(Y\).
\[g^{-1}(y)=\sigma y+\mu\]
Then:
\[f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
    f_X(\sigma y+\mu)|\sigma| & y\in A \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{y^2}{2}\right) & y\in\mathbb{R} \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Then through the uniqueness of PDFs we can see that \(Y\sim N(0,1)\).
\subsection{Two-dimensional Transformations}
Suppose that the transformation \(T:(x_1,x_2)\mapsto(y_1,y_2)\) exists and is injective. Then \(T\) has an inverse:
\[T^{-1}=H:(y_1,y_2)\mapsto(x_1,x_2)\]
\[H(y_1,y_2)=\begin{pmatrix}
    H_1(y_1,y_2) \\
    H_2(y_1,y_2)
\end{pmatrix}\]
The Jacobian of \(H\) is:
\[J_H=\begin{pmatrix}
    \pf{H_1}{y_1} & \pf{H_1}{y_2} \\
    \pf{H_2}{y_1} & \pf{H_2}{y_2} 
\end{pmatrix}\]
Suppose \((X_1,X_2)^T\) is a bivariate random variable joint pdf \(f_{X_1,X_2}(x_1,x_2)\) and support \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2\). If \(T:A\to\mathbb{R}^2\) is injective then \((Y_1,Y_2)=T(X_1,X_2)\) has joint pdf:
\[f_{Y_1,Y_2}(y_1,y_2)=\begin{cases}
    |\det J_H|f_{X_1,X_2}(H_1,H_2) & (y_1,y_2)\in T(A) \\
    0& \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Note that this extends into higher dimensions by simply increasing the dimensions of all vectors, functions and Jacobian.

\section{Introduction to Statistics}
Statistics is the science of identifying and quantifying structure of a population of interest using information from a suitable sample. Statistics comes in 3 main types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Descriptive
    \item Inferential
    \item Predictive
\end{itemize}
In this module we will primarily focus on inferential statistics. We will also examine methods of statistics which can get quite theoretical so it is important to remember that these methods are motivated by solving real problems in industry.
\subsection{Process of Statistical Inference}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Observe / Collect some sample data \(x_1,\dots,x_n\). We often use vector notation to represent these: \(\underline{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\).
    \item Our objective is to make inference about the population we have sampled from, accounting for uncertainty due to randomness in the sample selected.
    \item View each \(x_i\) as an observation of random variable \(X_i\). We again use vector notation: \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)\).
    \item We assume some probability distribution (model) for each \(X_i\). Often the \(X_i\) are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d). In particular we will assume some parametric model:
    \[X_i\sim \mathcal{D}(\theta)\]
    \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^p\) is some parameter and \(\mathcal{D}\) is some distribution with density or mass function \(f(x\mid\theta)\) determined by \(\theta\).
\end{itemize}
The set of possible values for \(\theta\) is called the parameter space and is often written \(\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^p\)
\subsection{Estimators}
Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\sim \mathcal{D}(\theta)\). Consider a function \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\), then appropriate choices of \(T(\underline{X})\) give us estimators of \(\theta\) or functions of \(\theta\). \(T(\underline{x})\) is known as the estimate corresponding to the estimator \(T(\underline{X})\). \(T(\underline{X})\) is itself a random variable which is called a statistic, \(T(\underline{X})\) therefore has a probability distribution known as the sampling distribution.
\subsubsection{Examples of estimators}
\begin{align*}
    &T(\underline{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum^n_{i=1}x_i=\bar{x} && \text{(Sample mean)} \\
    &T(\underline{x})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum^n_{i=1}(x_i-\bar{x})^2 && \text{(Sample variance)}
\end{align*}
Note that \(T(\underline{X})=\bar{X}\) is an estimator of \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) and given data \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), \(T(\underline{x})=\bar{x}\) is the observed estimated of \(\mathbb{E}[X]\).

\section{Properties of Estimators}
An estimator is a statistic \(T(\underline{X})\) used to estimate a quantity of interest. We therefore want \(T(\underline{X})\) to have "nice" properties.
\subsection{Bias}
We define the bias of \(T(\underline{X})\) to be:
\[\text{Bias}(T(\underline{X});\;\theta)=\mathbb{E}[T(\underline{X})]-\theta\]
If \(\text{Bias}(T(\underline{X}); \theta)=0\) then we say \(T(\underline{X})\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\). All this means is that \(\mathbb{E}[T]=\theta\).
\subsubsection*{Example}
Suppose \(X_1,\dots,X_n\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\). We want to estimate \(\mu\): Let \(T(\underline{X})=\bar{X}\), then:
\[\bar{X}\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}], \text{Var}[\bar{X}])\]
\[\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\right]=\frac{1}{n}n\mu=\mu\]
So we know that \(T\) is an unbiased estimator.
\[\text{Var}[\bar{X}]=\text{Var}\left[\frac{1}{n}(X_1+\cdots+X_n)\right]=\frac{1}{n^2}(\text{Var}[X_1]+\cdots\text{Var}[X_n])\]
\[=\frac{n}{n^2}=\frac{1}{n}\]
\[\therefore\]
\[\bar{X}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\frac{1}{n}\right)\]
In general to find an unbiased estimator we make an obvious guess, then check its bias and try to adjust if possible. Note that it may not always be trivial or even possible to do this.
\subsection{Precision and MSE}
The mean squared error is a measure of how precise the estimator is:
\[MSE(T(\underline{X}),\,\theta)=\mathbb{E}[(T(\underline{X})-\theta)^2]\]
Often we will just write \(MSE(T)\) when \(\underline{X}\) and \(\theta\) are clear from context. A useful result for actually computing this error is:
\[MSE(T)=\text{Var}(T)+(\text{Bias}(T))^2\]
This means that if you want to have a "better" estimator by making the \(MSE\) small, you can either make the bias small or make the variance small. This is an example of a bias-variance trade off problem: sometimes a biased estimator may be preferred to an unbiased one because it's variance is much smaller.
\subsection{Efficiency}
Let \(T_1(\underline{X})\) and \(T_2(\underline{X})\) be 2 estimators for \(\theta\). If \(MSE(T_1)<MSE(T_2)\) then we say that \(T_1\) is more efficient than \(T_2\).
\subsubsection*{Example}
Let:
\begin{align*}
    &X\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,4) && Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,9) \\
    &U=\frac{1}{2}(X+Y) && V=\frac{1}{3}(X+2Y)
\end{align*}
Where \(U,V\) are estimators of \(\theta\).
\[\mathbb{E}[U]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta\implies \text{Bias}(U,\theta)=0\]
\[\mathbb{E}[V]=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}[X]+\frac{2}{3}\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta\implies \text{Bias}(V,\theta)=0\]
\[\text{Var}(U)=\text{Cov}(U,U)=\text{Cov}\left(\frac{1}{2}(X+Y),\,\frac{1}{2}(X+Y)\right)\]
\[=\frac{1}{4}(\text{Var}[X]+\text{Var}[Y]+2\text{Cov}(X,Y))=\frac{13}{4}\]
\[\text{Var}[V]=\text{Cov}[V,V]=\text{Cov}\left(\frac{1}{3}(X+2Y),\, \frac{1}{3}(X+2Y)\right)\]
\[=\frac{1}{9}(\text{Var}[X]+4\text{Var}[Y]+4\text{Cov}(X,Y))=\frac{40}{9}\]
\[\frac{13}{4}<\frac{40}{9}\]
So \(U\) is more efficient than \(V\) because it has a lower mean squared error. How could we have realised \(U\) was better than \(V\) without doing all of these calculations? Well we know that \(X\) has a lower variance than \(Y\), so if we were weighting our variables, we would want more \(X\) than \(Y\).
\subsection{Consistency}
Recall that \(T(\underline{X})\) is a random variable and has some sampling distribution. We may wish to consider what happens to this distribution as the size of our sample increases. Let \(T_n(\theta)\) be an estimator of \(\theta\) with sample size \(n\), then \(T_n(\underline{X})\) is said to be weakly consistent to \(\theta\) if for all \(\varepsilon>0\):
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P[|T_n-\theta|>\varepsilon]=0\]
Notice this is the definition of converging in probability. Generally, consistency tells us about the location of a sampling distribution. Note that as a consequence:
\[MSE(T)\to 0\,\text{ as }n\to\infty\]
is a sufficient but not necessary condition for weak consistency. As a result of this, if we have an unbiased estimator it is sufficient to show:
\[MSE(T)=\text{Var}[T]\to 0\]

\section{Maximum Likelihood Estimation}
\subsection{The Likelihood Function}
If we have observations \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) from random variables \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) then we can write the joint density/mass function \(f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\), where \(\theta\) is a parameter of \(f\). The likelihood function is defined:
\[\mathcal{L}(\theta)=f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\]
Written as a function of \(\theta\) with \(\underline{x}\) being some fixed observed sample.
\subsection{Maximum Likelihood Estimates}
The "best" value of \(\theta\) is the one that maximises this likelihood function:
\[\hat{\theta}=\max_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\]
We call this value \(\hat{\theta}\), the maximum likelihood estimate (mle). It is often beneficial to instead work with the logarithm of \(\mathcal{L}\):
\[\ell(\theta)=\log(\mathcal{L}(\theta))\]
Note that this log transformation does not change the value \(\hat{\theta}\).
\subsubsection{Properties of likelihood functions and estimates}
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid, then \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\prod^n_{i=1}f_{X_i}(x_i\mid\theta)\).
    \item The value \(\hat{\theta}\) that maximises the function \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\), also maximises \(\ell(\theta)\).
    \item If \(\phi=g(\theta)\) then \(\hat{\phi}=g(\hat{\theta})\) for sufficiently nice functions \(g\). Throughout this module this property will mostly be assumed.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Score Equations}
The way we find this maximum in practice is by solving a score equation of the form:
\[\pf{\ell}{\theta}=0\]
Note that this method will work for situations of "regular form". What this means in practice is that we have to be careful that our derivative exists and is finite in the domain that we are considering - for example, this equation breaks down if our maximum is at an endpoint of our domain. These cases are called irregular. In regular cases we can prove that we have a maximum by calculus:
\[\pf{^2\ell}{\theta^2}|_{\hat{\theta}}<0\]
\subsubsection*{Example}
Suppose that \(X_1,\dots,X_n\iid\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2\) where \(\underline{\theta}=(\mu,\sigma^2)\) are parameters. Now take \(\underline{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) to be a set of observed data, then:
\[f_{X_i}(x_i\mid \mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(x_i-\mu)^2\right)\]
Then:
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{L}(\mu,\sigma^2)=\prod^n_{i=1}f_{X_i}(x_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2)&&\text{(By independence)}  
\end{align*}
\[\mathcal{L}(\mu,\sigma^2)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum^n_{i=1}(x_i-\mu)^2\right)\]
We can find the mle's \(\hat{\mu}\) and \(\hat{\sigma^2}\) by jointly maximising this over \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\). This gets us:
\begin{align*}
    &\hat{\mu}=\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^n_{i=1}x_i \\
    &\hat{\sigma^2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^n_{i=1}(x_i-\bar{x})^2
\end{align*}
