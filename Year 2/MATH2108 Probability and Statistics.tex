\section{Bivariate Probability Distributions}
\subsection{Background}
Recall from Year 1, the notion of random variables and the sample space \(\Omega\). Consider the random variable \(X\):
\[X:\Omega\to S\]
If \(S\) is countable then \(X\) is discrete. If \(S\) is uncountable then \(X\) is continuous. Considering the likelihood of each event in the sample space for a discrete random variable, gives the probability mass function. We say that this pmf defines the distribution. We can define the cumulative distribution function to assign meaning to the random variable:
\[F_X:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\]
\[F_X(x)=P[X\leq x],\ x\in\mathbb{R}\]
If \(X\) is continuous, we can further define the probability density function:
\[f_X(x)=\df{F_X(x)}{x}\]
\[\implies F_x(x)=\int^x_{-\infty}f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
Whilst CDFs can be defined for discrete variables, it is important that our outcomes in this case are ordinal. If they are not then there is no meaning to the CDF.
\subsection{Bivariate Distributions}
\subsubsection*{Definitions}
Often, we wish to consider more than one variable at the same time. In these cases, the variables may be correlated. Rather than considering each variable separately, we would like to consider their joint probability distributions. At the beginning of this module we are only going to lok at bivariate probability distributions.
\\
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables. Then the joint CDF of \(X\) and \(Y\) is:
\[F_{X,Y}(x,y)=P[X\leq x,\, Y\leq y]\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}\]
\[F_{X,Y}:\mathbb{R}^2\to [0,1]\]
Suppose \(F_{X,Y}\) is a joint CDF, then if there exists a function \(f_{X,Y}\) such that:
\[F_{X,Y}(x,y)=\int^y_{-\infty}\int^x_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(u,v)\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v\]
Then \(f_{X,Y}\) is the joint PDF of \(X\) and \(Y\), with:
\[f_{X,Y}=\pf{^2F_{X,Y}(x,y)}{x\partial y}\]
A bivariate PDF is a surface over the \((x,y)\) plane. This means that calculated probabilities are volumes under the surface, analogous to in 1D where probabilities are areas under the curve.
\subsubsection*{Conditions of existence}
For a joint PDF to be valid it must satisfy the following conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\geq 0\quad \forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\)
    \item \(\int^\infty_{-\infty}\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(u,v)\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}v=1\)
    \item \(\forall\,C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2:\)
    \[P[(X,Y)\in C]=\iint_{(x,y)\in C}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Marginal Distributions}
We can also consider \(X\) and \(Y\) from a joint PDF separately. We may only be interested in one of the variables but need to acknowledge its connection to the other. The marginal PDFs of \(X\) and \(Y\) for a bivariate probability distribution are defined as:
\begin{align*}
    &f_X(x)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}y\\
    &f_Y(y)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x
\end{align*}
Likewise:
\begin{align*}
    &F_X(x)=F_{X,Y}(x,\infty)\\
    &F_Y(y)=F_{X,Y}(\infty,y)
\end{align*}
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    e^{-x-y} & x>0,\, y>0\\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Then:
\[f_X(x)=\int^\infty_{0}e^{-x-y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int^t_0e^{-x-y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-e^{-x-y}\right]^t_0\]
\[=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-e^{-x-t}+e^{-x}\right)\]
\[=e^{-x}\]
Hence:
\[f_X(x)=\begin{cases}
    e^{-x} & x>0\\
    0& \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Due to symmetry, the same argument applies for the marginal PDF of \(Y\).
\subsection{Functions of \(X\) and \(Y\)}
Often we will have a function of random variables, for example:
\[Z=g(X,Y)\]
Where \(X\) and \(Y\) have the joint pdf \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\). In this case we may wish to determine the CDF of this function:
\[F_{Z}(z)=P[Z\leq z]=\iint_{\{(x,y):\; g(x.y)\leq z\}}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
Note that this integral is sometimes very difficult or analytically impossible to solve. We will return to functions of random variables later in the module.
\subsection{Independence}
Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be continuous random variables. \(X\) and \(Y\) are said to be independent if:
\[P[X\leq x\cap Y\leq y]=P[X\leq x]\cdot P[Y\leq y]\]
That is:
\[F_{X,Y}(x,y)=F_{X}(x)F_{Y}(y),\quad \forall (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\]
In words this means that having knowledge of the distribution of \(X\), tells us nothing about the distribution of \(Y\). We can formalise this into a somewhat more useful condition:
\[X,Y\text{ are independent}\iff f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)\]
Under some conditions, namely that \(F_{X,Y}\) is differentiable. In this module we do not need to worry about these conditions since all functions we encounter will be suitable "nice". If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then it follows:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=f_X(x)\]
\[f_{Y|X}(y|x)=f_Y(y)\]
\subsubsection*{Example}
Determine if the random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent given their joint pdf:
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    8xy & 0<y<x<1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[f_{X}(x)=\int^x_08xy\,\text{d}y=4x^3\]
\[f_{X}(x)=\begin{cases}
    4x^3 & 0<x<1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[f_{Y}(y)=\int^1_y8xy\,\text{d}x=4y(1-y^2)\]
\[f_{Y}(y)=\begin{cases}
    4y(1-y^2) & 0<y<1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Clearly we have:
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)\ne f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)\]
for some value \((x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) so \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. We could have seen this before calculating however, by just checking the conditions of the joint pdf and noticing a relationship between the values of \(X\) and \(Y\).

\section{Further Bi and Multivariate Probability Distributions}
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent and continuous with pdf's \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\) respectively. We want  to consider \(Z=X+Y\).
\[F_Z(z)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}F_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
It further follows that:
\[f_{X+Y}(z)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\subsection{The Convolution Theorem}
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent and continuous with pdf's \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\) respectively. We want  to consider \(Z=X+Y\).
\[f_{X+Y}(z)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_X(x)f_Y(z-y)\,\text{d}x\]
The function \(f_Z\) is called the convolution of \(f_X\) and \(f_Y\).
\subsection{Functions of Independent Variables}
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables then the functions \(g_1(X)\) and \(g_2(Y)\) are independent for any "nice" functions \(g_1,g_2\). We do not need to define what constitutes a "nice" function in this module.
\subsection{More Than Two Random Variables}
Joint probability distributions may also be known as multivariate probability distributions. Throughout this module we will denote random variables as \(X_1,X_2,...,X_n\). Their joint CDF:
\[F_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=P[X_1\leq x_1,\dots,X_n\leq x_n]\]
Their joint pdf is:
\[f_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\pf{F_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)}{x_1\partial x_2\cdots \partial x_n}\]
Marginals may be defined for subsets as well as single random variables:
\[F_{X_1}(x_1)=F_{X_1,\dots, X_n}(x_1,\infty,\cdots,\infty)\]
\[F_{X_1,X_2,X_4}(x_1,x_2,x_4)=F_{X_1,\dots, X_n}(x_1,x_2,\infty,x_4,\cdots,\infty)\]
Similarly:
\[f_{X_1}(x_1)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots, x_n)\,\text{d}x_2\cdots\text{d}x_n\]
This notation gets quite cumbersome very quickly so we often use vector notation with multivariate distributions. We write \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\) and \(\underline{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\). Then:
\begin{align*}
    &F_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=P[\underline{X}\leq \underline{x}] \\
    &f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})=\pf{F_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})}{x_1\partial x_2\cdots \partial x_n} \\
    &P[\underline{X}\in C]=\int_{\underline{x}\in C}f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x}),\quad C\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n
\end{align*}
\subsection{Mutual Independence}
We have mutual independence if:
\[F_{\underline{X}_{(r)}}(\underline{x}_{(r)})=\prod^{|\mathcal{X}_r|}_{j=1}F_{\underline{X}_{(r)_j}}(\underline{x}_{(r)_j})\]
For each subset \(\mathcal{X}_{(r)}\) of \(\{X_1,X_2,\dots\}\). However this kind of sucks, so consider it in the same way as with two variables. We have independence if the joint pdf can be expressed as the product of all of the marginal pdf's. In words, we require that no finite combination of any random variables provides any information about the other variables. Mutual independence is a stronger condition than pairwise independence. The collection of random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are said to be independent and identically distributed if \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are mutually independent.

\section{Conditional Distributions}
\subsection{Conditional PDFs}
Suppose \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) is the joint pdf of the random variables \(X\) and \(Y\). If \(f_Y(y)\) is the marginal pdf of \(Y\) then the conditional pdf of \(X\) given \(Y\) is:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
for \(y\) such that \(f_Y(y)>0\). This implies that:
\[f_X(x)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X|Y}(x|y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
In some cases this form can be easier to calculate than using the joint pdf. Similarly this works for multivariate random variables:
\[f_{X_2,\dots,X_n|X_1}(x_2,\dots,x_n|x_1)=\frac{f_{X_1,\dots,X_n}(x_1,\dots,x_n)}{f_{X_1}(x_1)}\]
\subsection{Calculating Probabilities}
If the random variables \(X\) and \((Y\) are such that \(f_{X|Y}(x|y)\) is the pdf of \(X\) conditional on \(Y\), then for \(C\subseteq\mathbb{R}\):
\[P[X\in C| Y=y]=\int_C f_{X|Y}(x|y)\,\text{d}x\]
\[F_{X|Y}(x|y)=\int^x_{-\infty}f_{X|Y}(u|y)\,\text{d}u\]
Do not overthink this it just the same operation as calculating for a single variable. For the standard pdf of \(X\), we would compute \(P[X<1]\) as:
\[P[X<1]=\int^1_{-\infty}f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
For the conditional \(P[X<1|Y=y]\) we calculate in almost exactly the same way:
\[P[X<1|Y=y]=\int^1_{-\infty}f_{X|Y}(x|y)\,\text{d}x\]
Treat \(y\) as a constant since that is exactly what it is in this context.
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    x+y & 0\leq x,y\leq 1 \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Then:
\[f_Y(y)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\]
\[f_Y(y)=\int^1_0x+y\ \text{d}x\]
\[f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{2}+y&0\leq y\leq 1 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
If \(y\notin[0,1]\), then \(f_Y(y)=0\) and so the conditional pdf is not defined. Otherwise:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{x+y}{\frac{1}{2}+y}\]
For \(x,y\in[0,1]\). We can now compute conditional probabilities:
\[P[X\geq \frac{1}{2}|Y=\frac{1}{3}]=\int^1_{\frac{1}{2}}f_{X|Y=\frac{1}{3}}(x|y=\frac{1}{3})\,\text{d}x\]
\[=\int^1_{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{2x+\frac{2}{3}}{\frac{5}{3}}\,\text{d}x\]
\[=0.65\]
\subsection{Independence}
All of the following sentences are equivalent (provided all necessary functions exist):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent
    \item \(f_{X|Y}(x|y)=f_X(x),\ \forall x\in\mathbb{R},\  f_Y(y)>0\)
    \item \(f_{Y|X}(y|x)=f_Y(y),\ \forall y\in\mathbb{R},\  f_X(x)>0\)
\end{enumerate}

\section{Expectation}
\subsection{Definition}
For a single continuous random variable \(X\), with pdf \(f_X(x)\), we know it's expectation:
\[\mathbb{E}(X)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
Moreover:
\[\mathbb{E}(g(X))=\int^\infty_{-\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
We should always be mindful of the distribution to which our expectation relates. We can also consider expectation for the continuous random variables \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\). If \(\underline{X}\) is a multivariate random variable with pdf \(f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})\) and \(g:A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) is a suitable function, then the expectation of \(g(\underline{X})\) is:
\[\mathbb{E}(g(\underline{X}))=\int\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}g(\underline{x})f_{\underline{X}}(\underline{x})\,\text{d}x\]
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{2}xy & 0\leq x\leq y\leq 2 \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
\[\mathbb{E}(2X+Y)=\int^2_0\int^y_0(2x+y)(\frac{1}{2}xy)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^2_0\frac{y^4}{3}+\frac{y^4}{4}\,\text{d}y=\frac{56}{15}\]
To help you find the appropriate bounds of integration, consider what happens when you "squish" the function to the axis after integrating over one variable.
\subsection{Linearity of Expectations}
Since expectations are defined via integrals - and integrals are linear - we have:
\[\mathbb{E}(ag_1(\underline{X})+bg_2(\underline{X})+c)=a\mathbb{E}(g_1(\underline{X}))+b\mathbb{E}(g_2(\underline{X}))+c\]
Where \(g_1,g_2:A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}\). Additionally, for any collection of random variables:
\[\mathbb{E}\left(\sum^n_{i=1}X_i\right)=\sum^n_{i=1}\mathbb{E}(X_i)\]
Note that this property holds without any conditions on independence.
\subsection{Expectations of Functions}
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables, then for any functions \(g,h\):
\[\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[(g(X)]\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\]
This comes from the integral definition, and the key property of independence.
\[\int^\infty_{-\infty}\int^\infty_{-\infty}g(x)h(y)f_X(x)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\int^\infty_{-\infty}h(y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\subsection{Covariance and Correlation}
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables. The covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\) is:
\[\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]\]
This is clearly \(0\) if \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent but the converse is not true.
\subsubsection{Properties of covariance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\text{Cov}(X,X)=\text{Var}(X)\)
    \item \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\text{Cov}(Y,X)\)
    \item \(\text{Cov}(aX+b,\,cY+d)=ac\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\)
\end{itemize}
For random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_m\) and \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) we have:
\[\text{Cov}\left[\left(\sum^m_{i=1}a_iX_i\right),\,\left(\sum^n_{i=1}b_jY_j\right)\right]=\sum^m_{i=1}\sum^n_{j=1}a_ib_j\,\text{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)\]
Using this it is not too hard to see that:
\[\text{Var}\left(\sum^n_{i=1}a_iX_i\right)=\underbrace{\sum^n_{i=1}a_i^2\,\text{Var}(X_i)}_{i=j}+2\underbrace{\sum_{1\leq i< j\leq n}a_ia_j\,\text{Cov}(X_i,X_j)}_{i\ne j}\]
Where the two comes from the symmetry of the variance-covariance matrix, which in turn exists because of commutativity. This means that without loss of generality we can set \(i<j\).
\subsubsection*{Example}
\begin{align*}
    &\mathbb{E}[X]=1 && \text{Var}[X]=2 \\
    &\mathbb{E}[Y]=2 && \text{Var}[Y]=4 \\
    &\text{Cov}(X,Y)= 1
\end{align*}
We wish to compute Cov\((2X+Y,\,2Y-X)\):
\[\text{Cov}\left(\sum^m_{i=1}a_iX_i,\, \sum^n_{j=1}b_jY_j\right)=\sum^m_{i=1}\sum^n_{j=1}a_ib_j\, \text{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)\]
\begin{align*}
    \text{Cov}(2X+Y,\,2Y-X)=&\\ 
    &\underbrace{(2\cdot2)\text{Cov}(X,Y)}_{2X\cdot2Y} \\
    +&\underbrace{(1\cdot2)\text{Var}(Y)}_{Y\cdot 2Y} \\
    -&(1\cdot2)\text{Var}(X) \\
    -&(1\cdot1)\text{Cov}(X,Y)\\
    =&3\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)+2\,\text{Var}(Y)-2\,\text{Var}(X)=7
\end{align*}
\subsection{Variance-Covariance Matrix}
For the multivariate random variable \(\underline{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\), the variance-covariance matrix of \(\underline{X}\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix whose \((r,s)\) entry is given by \(\text{Cov}(X_r,X_s)\). It is denoted \(\Sigma\).
\[\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}
    \text{Var}(X_1) & \text{Cov}(X_1,X_2) & \cdots & \text{Cov}(X_1,X_n) \\
    \text{Cov}(X_2,X_1) & \text{Var} (X_2) & \cdots & \text{Cov}(X_2,X_n) \\
    \vdots & \cdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    \text{Cov}(X_n,X_1) & \text{Cov}(X_n, X_2) & \cdots & \text{Var}(X_n)
\end{pmatrix}\]
Note that in order to calculate the variance-covariance matrix, we need the following information:
\begin{align*}
    &\mathbb{E}[X] && \mathbb{E}[X^2] \\
    &\mathbb{E}[Y] && \mathbb{E}[Y^2] \\
    &\mathbb{E}[XY]
\end{align*}
\subsubsection{An important lemma}
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables such that \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0\) and \(\text{Var}(X)=\text{Var}(Y)=1\) then:
\[|\mathbb{E}[XY]|\leq 1\]
You might be wondering when we have those conditions for this lemma to be true, and it turns out this is always the case after we have standardised the random variables. This is a useful operation we will do a lot so this lemma comes into effect more times than you would think.
\subsection{Cauchy-Schwartz Inequality}
For any pair of random variables \(X\) and \(Y\):
\[|\text{Cov}(X,Y)|^2\leq \text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)\]
The real power of this statement is in the word "any".
\subsection{Correlation}
Sometimes we prefer to use a measure that is independent of the magnitude of the data. One such measure is the Pearson correlation:
\[\rho(X,Y)=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)}}\]
This is dimensionless and provides a measure of linear association between \(X\) and \(Y\).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(|\rho|\leq 1\)
    \item If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then \(\rho(X,Y)=0\) but the converse is not true.
    \item For constants \(a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}\):
    \[\rho(aX+b,\, cY+d)=\begin{cases}
        \rho(X,Y)& ac>0 \\
        -\rho(X,Y)&ac<0
    \end{cases}\]
\end{itemize}

\section{Conditional Expectation and Variance}
\subsection{Conditional Expectation}
Recall that the conditional pdf of random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) is given by:
\[f_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
subject to \(f_Y(y)>0\). Now suppose that \(X\) and \(Y\) are random variables. The conditional expectation of \(X\) given \(Y=y\) is:
\[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x|y)\,\text{d}x\]
Notice that this is a one dimensional integral since we are conditioning on \(Y=y\) being constant. We can also assume \(Y\) remains a random variable by substituting \(y\) for \(Y\) in our expectation.
\subsubsection*{Example}
\[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{4x}{y} & 0\leq x< y \leq 1 \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
We wish to calculate \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]\).
\begin{align*}
    f_Y(y)&=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{4x}{y}\,\text{d}x \\\\
    &=\int^y_0\frac{4x}{y}\,\text{d}x \\\\
    &=\left[\frac{2x^2}{y}\right]^y_0 \\\\
    &=\begin{cases}
        2y & 0\leq y \leq 1 \\
        0&\text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}
\[f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
When \(0<y\leq 1\) this is defined and we have:
\[f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=\begin{cases}
    \frac{2x}{y^2} & 0<x<y \\
    0&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
Finally we have:
\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x\mid y)\,\text{d}x\]
\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=\frac{2}{3}y\]
If we were instead looking for \(Y\) to remain a random variable then:
\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\frac{2}{3}Y\].
\subsubsection{Functions of \(X\)}
We can also defined conditional random variables such as \(\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y]\) and indeed for any suitable function \(f(X)\) we can define \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]\). This naturally leads us to define conditional variances:
\[\text{Var}[X|Y=y]=\mathbb{E}[X^2|Y=y]-(\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y])^2\]
\subsection{The Tower Property}
Because \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y]\) is in itself a random variable, we can take the expectation of it. If we do this, our expectation will be considering the marginal of \(Y\). When dealing with lots of these expectations, it may make sense to use the older notation \(\mathbb{E}_Y\) to denote which pdf our expectation is taken with respect to. For any pair of random variables \(X\) and \(Y\):
\[\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]\]
Note that this holds for both discrete and continuous random variables and even if \(X\) and \(Y\) are not the same type. This is a very important result that gets used all the time.
\subsubsection{Proof of the tower property}
Consider \(\mathbb{E}[X|Y]\):
\[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x\mid y)\,\text{d}x = g(Y)\]
This is a random variable and a function of \(Y\).
\[\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]\]
\[\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]=\int^\infty_{\infty}g(y)f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\left\{\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X|Y}(x\mid y)\,\text{d}x\right\}\,\text{d}y\]
Note that:
\[f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\]
\[\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}xf_{X,Y}(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\]

\subsubsection{Continuous case example}
Suppose \(Y\sim\text{Gamma}(2,\theta)\) and \(X|Y=y\sim\text{Exp}(y)\). Then:
\[\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]=\frac{1}{y}\]
\[\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{y}\right]\]
\[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{Y}\right]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{1}{y}f_Y(y)\,\text{d}y\] 
\[=\int^\infty_0\frac{1}{y}\theta^2ye^{-\theta y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\theta\int^\infty_0\theta e^{-\theta y}\,\text{d}y\]
\[=\theta\]
Since the remaining integral is a pdf and so integrates to \(1\) over its entire domain.
\subsection{Conditional Probabilities of Events}
If \(X\) is a random variable and \(A\) is some event of interest then:
\[\mathbb{P}[A]=\int^\infty_{-\infty}\mathbb{P}[A|X=x]f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
This is analogous to the law of total probabilities in the discrete world. In particular we can consider the random variable:
\[Y=\begin{cases}
    1 & \text{If event A occurs} \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\]
An extension of this, and another way of proving an old result, is that if \(X\) and \(Y\) are random variables, then:
\[f_Y(y)=\int^\infty_{-\infty}f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)f_X(x)\,\text{d}x\]
