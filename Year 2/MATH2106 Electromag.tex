\section{Electrostatics}
\subsection{A Word on Notation}
Most of electromagnetism takes place in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), although unless we are taking a vector product, there is no reason for it to not apply in higher dimensions. Vectors should always be underlined and in a piece of standard physics notation, the same character used but not underlined represents the magnitude of that vector:
\[r=|\underline{r}|\]
Unit vectors are denoted with a hat:
\[\underline{r}=r\,\underline{\hat{r}}\]
Most of electromagnetism will take place in Cartesian coordinates. The Cartesian unit vectors are:
\[\underline{\hat{x}},\ \underline{\hat{y}},\ \underline{\hat{z}}\]
\subsection{Charges}
The SI unit of charge is Coulombs (C). These are defined such that the charge of the electron is:
\[q_e=-e=-1.60\dots\times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}\]
You will not be asked to remember this. In these lectures, a small \(q\) will be used for charges that generate electric fields and a capital \(Q\) will be used for a "test charge" that has forces acting upon it from existing in a field. We are generally interested in the force caused by \(q\) and acting on \(Q\). This force is given to us via Coulombs law:
\[\underline{F}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{\scriptr^2}\hat{\underline{\scriptr}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|^3}(\underline{r}-\underline{r}')\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/singlechargeforce.png}
    \caption{A test charge \(Q\) is acted on by a field generated from \(q\).}
\end{figure}
\subsubsection{Observations of Coulombs law}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The magnitude of the force decreases quadratically with the distance between the two charges.
    \item Force is proportional to the product of the charges.
    \item If \(q\) and \(Q\) have the same sign, then the force points away from the field. This is repulsion.
    \item If \(q\) and \(Q\) have opposite signs, then the force points towards the field. This is attraction.
    \item \(\varepsilon_0\) is the vacuum permittivity and is approximately:
    \[\varepsilon_0\approx 8.854\times 10^{-12}\,\frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{Nm}^2} \]
    You do not need to memorise this.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Multiple Charges}
If the force acting on \(Q\) is generated by \(n\) charges \(q_i\), then it is equal to the sum of the forces by the individual charges:
\[\underline{F}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum^n_{i=1}\frac{q_iQ}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}_i'|^3}(\underline{r}-\underline{r}_i')\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/multiplechargeforce.png}
    \caption{A test charge \(Q\), acted on by force generated from multiple charges \(q_i\).}
\end{figure}
Notice that we can take \(Q\) out of the sum and write:
\[\underline{F}=Q\underline{E}\]
Where \(\underline{E}(\underline{r})\) is called the electric field and is defined as:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum^n_{i=1}\frac{q_i}{\scriptr^2}\hat{\underline{\scriptr}}\]
\subsection{Continuous Charge Distributions}
Coulombs law doesn't restrict us to just working with discrete sums (although in reality electrons are point charges). In particular if we have a field with lots of charges we may need to look at continuous charge distributions:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{r})=\int\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{\scriptr^2}\hat{\underline{\scriptr}}\,\text{d}q\]
The actual form of this integral will depend on where the charges are located. For example we could have a line or surface integral. If the charge is concentrated on a curve, then define the charge density \(\lambda\):
\[\text{d}q=\lambda \text{d}\ell\]
In this case \(\ell\) is some length parameter along the curve.
\subsubsection{Line charges}
We can construct the electric field for a continuous charge distributions where our charges form a line. Notice that the force acting on a test charge from this line decreases with distance and indeed if the distance from the line is significantly larger than the line itself then it just appears like a point charge at the origin.
\subsubsection{Spherical coordinate recap}
Recall the definitions for spherical coordinates:
\begin{align*}
    &x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi \\
    &y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi \\
    &z=r\cos\theta
\end{align*}
Where:
\begin{align*}
    &\theta\in[0,\pi] && \text{is the polar angle} \\
    &\phi\in [0,2\pi) && \text{is the azimuthal angle}
\end{align*}
Assume we wish to integrate a function \(f\) over a volume \(V\):
\[\int_V f(\underline{r})\,\text{d}\tau\]
In Cartesian coordinates this volume element is:
\[\text{d}\tau=\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}z\]
In spherical coordinates the volume element is:
\[\text{d}\tau=r^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
Consider a surface integral of a sphere of radius \(R\):
\[\int_Sf(\underline{r})\,\text{d}a\]
\[\text{d}a=R^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
The surface area is calculated as:
\[A=\int_S\text{d}a=\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0R^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[=2\pi R^2\int^\pi_0\sin\theta\,\text{d}\theta=4\pi R^2\]
Likewise we can calculate the volume of a ball of radius \(R\):
\[V=\int_B\,\text{d}\tau=\int^R_0\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0r^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[=4\pi\int^R_0r^2\,\text{d}r=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\]
\subsubsection{Charges on the surface of a sphere}
Consider a sphere of radius \(R\) with a uniform surface charge \(\sigma\). We wish to know the field at a distance \(r\) from the centre of the sphere. We can choose our axis such that:
\[\underline{r}=r\underline{\hat{z}}\]
A point on the sphere has position vector:
\[r'=R\sin\theta'\cos\phi'\underline{\hat{x}}+R\sin\theta'\sin\phi'\underline{\hat{y}}+R\cos\theta'\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}=\underline{r}-\underline{r}'=-R\sin\theta'\cos\phi'\underline{\hat{x}}-R\sin\theta'\sin\phi'\underline{\hat{y}}+(r-R\cos\theta')\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\scriptr^2=\underline{\scriptr}\cdot\underline{\scriptr}=R^2+r^2-2rR\cos\theta'\]
By symmetry of \(\sin\phi'\) and \(\cos\phi'\) we have that:
\begin{align*}
    E_x=0 && E_y=0
\end{align*}
Finally we need to compute \(E_z\), which is the most tedious integration of the entire module (apparently).
\[E_z=\frac{\sigma R^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0\frac{(r-R\cos\theta')\sin\theta'}{(R^2+r^2-2rR\cos\theta')^{3/2}}\,\text{d}\phi'\,\text{d}\theta'\]
We can make a substitution to make this integral easier to solve:
\begin{align*}
    &u=\cos\theta' && \text{d}u=-\sin\theta'\,\text{d}\theta' \\
    &u(0)=1 && u(\pi)=1
\end{align*}
Then:
\[E_z=\frac{-\sigma R^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\int^{-1}_1\frac{r-Ru}{(R^2+r^2-2rRu)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}u\]
\[E_z=\frac{\sigma R(r^2-R^2)}{4\varepsilon_0 r^2}\left(\frac{1}{|r-R|}-\frac{1}{r+R}\right)-\frac{\sigma R^2}{4\varepsilon_0 r^2}(|r-R|+(r+R))\]
Consider the case \(r>R\):
\[E_z=\frac{\sigma R^2}{\varepsilon_0 r^2}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\]
And since the charge generating the field is spherically symmetric, then:
\[\underline{E}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\underline{r}\]
This means that outside the sphere, the field is exactly that of a point charge at the origin. if \(r<R\) then:
\[E_z\implies \underline{E}=\underline{0}\]
Inside the sphere, the electric charge is 0.
\subsubsection{General spherically symmetric charge densities}
If we have a spherically symmetric charge density \(\rho\) on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), that is that \(\rho\) depends only on \(r'\). Then:
\[\underline{E}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^\infty_0\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0\rho(r')\frac{\underline{\scriptr}}{\scriptr^{3/2}}r'^2\sin\theta'\,\text{d}\phi'\,\text{d}\theta'\,\text{d}r'\]
As in the case of the sphere surface charge:
\[E_x=E_y=0\]
\[E_z=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 r^3}\int^r_0\rho(r')r'^2\,\text{d}r'\,\underline{r}\]
What this is saying is that the field generated by a spherically symmetric charge distribution \(r\) from the centre of the distribution is the same as that generated by a point charge at the centre, with magnitude contained inside the ball of radius \(r\). The charge outside of the radius \(r\) has no influence on the field at \(r\).
\subsection{Electric Flux}
Graphically we can represent an electric field with field lines. These are tangent lines to the field at every point in space. we can imagine these field lines passing through a surface and this is defined to be the flux of the field across the surface:
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/Fieldlines.png}
    \caption{Field lines passing through a surface.}
\end{figure}
We can choose a unit normal \(\underline{\hat{n}}\) to our surface \(S\). We can define the flux of a vector field \(\underline{E}\) through a surface \(S\) as:
\[\Phi=\int_S\underline{E}\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a\]
\subsubsection{Some remarks}
\begin{itemize}
    \item It is typical to write \(\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a\) as \(\text{d}\underline{a}\).
    \item There are two choices for normal vectors and choosing between them means choosing in what direction flux is counted as positive.
    \item This formula only holds for surfaces where normal vectors can be found. This includes smooth and piecewise smooth surfaces. If a surface is piecewise smooth you can calculate the flux by summing all of the individual fluxes through the faces.
    \item The surface must be orientable.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection*{Example}
Find the flux of the electric field generated by a point charge at the origin, through a circular disc of radius \(R\) at heigh \(z=h>0\).
\[\underline{r}'=\underline{0}\implies \underline{\scriptr}=\underline{r}\]
\[\underline{r}=r\cos\theta\underline{\hat{x}}+r\sin\theta\underline{\hat{y}}+h\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\scriptr^2=r^2+h^2\]
By coulomb's law:
\[\underline{E}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{\scriptr^3}\underline{\scriptr}\]
Choose \(\underline{\hat{n}}=\underline{\hat{z}}\) to be the outward normal. Then:
\[\text{d}\underline{a}=\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a=\underline{\hat{z}}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}r\]
Now we can find the flux:
\[\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=E_z\,\text{d}a\]
\[\Phi=\int_S\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^R_0\int^{2\pi}_0\frac{h}{(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}r\]
\[=\frac{hq}{2\varepsilon_0}\int^R_0\frac{r}{(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}r\]
\[=\frac{-hq}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(r^2+h^2)^{1/2}}\right]^{r=R}_{r=0}\]
\[=\frac{hq}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{h}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+h^2}}\right)\]
\subsection{Volumes and Closed Surfaces}
Lets introduce some new notation. An arbitrarily shaped volume in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) will be called \(\mathcal{V}\) and it's surface \(\partial \mathcal{V}\). We will assume that \(\partial \mathcal{V}\) is piecewise smooth. The volume of \(\mathcal{V}\) will be denoted \(|\mathcal{V}|\). A particularly useful volume is the ball of radius \(R\) centred around \(\underline{r}_0\): We call this ball \(\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\) and it's surface \(\partial\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\). The flux through a closed surface us often written using a circulation integral:
\[\Phi=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}}\underline{E}\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}\]
It is convention to choose \(\underline{\hat{n}}\) facing outwards.
\subsection{Relating Flux and Charge}
Consider a point charge \(q\) generating a volume field  and its flux through a sphere of radius \(R\). We choose the coordinates such that the charge is on the \(z\) axis and the sphere is centred at the origin. We can choose \(q\) to be either inside or outside the sphere.
\[\underline{r}'=r'\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{r}=R\sin\theta\cos\phi\underline{\hat{x}}+R\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+R\cos\theta\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}=R\sin\theta\cos\phi\underline{\hat{x}}+R\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+(R\cos\theta-r')\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\scriptr^2=R^2+r'^2-2Rr'\cos\theta\]
\[\underline{\hat{n}}=\underline{\hat{r}}=\sin\theta\cos\phi\underline{\hat{x}}+\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+\cos\theta\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}=R-r'\cos\theta\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=(R-r'\cos\theta)R^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\phi\,\text{d}\theta\]
\[\implies \Phi=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0\frac{(R-r'\cos\theta)R^2\sin\theta}{(R^2+r'^2-2Rr'\cos\theta)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}\phi\,\text{d}\theta\]
\[\implies\Phi=\begin{cases}
    \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} & r'<R \\
    0 & r'>R
\end{cases}\]
If we instead have several point charges generating the field we get:
\[\Phi=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\sum_{j,\underline{r}_j\in \mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r_0)}}q_j\]
This looks much more complicated than it is. All this is saying is to find the total flux we just sum the all the charges inside the ball of radius \(R\). This means that charges outside the ball do not affect the flux. We usually use some shorthand notation to make this easier to write:
\[\Phi=\frac{q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}\]
Where the subscript represents all the charges enclosed. This statement still holds if the charge distribution is continuous:
\[q_{enc}=\int_{\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\rho(\underline{r}')\,\text{d}\tau'\]
\subsection{Flux Through Arbitrary Closed Surfaces}
Recall the mean value theorem for integration: If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is a continuous function, there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) such that:
\[\int^b_af(x)\,\text{d}x=(b-a)f(c)\]
There is also a 3D version of this equation, which takes the form: If \(f:\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\to \mathbb{R}\) is a continuous function then there exists an \(\underline{r}_1\in\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\) such that:
\[\int_{\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}f(\underline{r})\,\text{d}\tau=|\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)|f(\underline{r}_1)=\frac{4\pi}{3}R^3f(\underline{r}_1)\]
\subsubsection{The divergence theorem}
If \(\mathcal{V}\) is compact, \(\partial \mathcal{V}\) is piecewise smooth and \(\underline{E}\) is continuously differentiable then:
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}}\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=\int_{\mathcal{V}}\dive{E}\,\text{d}\tau\]
If we assume a continuous charge distribution \(\rho\), which generates a continuously differentiable field \(\underline{E}\) then:
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\underline{E}\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}=\int_{\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\dive{E}\,\text{d}\tau\]
The mean value theorem allows us to evaluate one side of this integral and reveals that there exists some \(\underline{r}_1\in\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\) such that:
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=|\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)|(\dive{E})(\underline{r}_1)\]
From our earlier calculations of the flux, we have:
\[\oint_{\partial \mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=\frac{q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}|\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)|\rho(\underline{r}'_1)\]
Equation both expressions for the integral tells us that there exists \(\underline{r}_1,\underline{r}'_1\in\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\) such that:
\[(\dive{E})(\underline{r}_1)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho(\underline{r}'_1)\]
\subsubsection{Gauss's Law}
We can take the above expression and examine what happens as we take the limit of our radius \(R\) to \(0\):
\begin{align*}
    &\lim_{R\to 0}\underline{r}_1=\lim_{R\to 0}\underline{r}'_1=\underline{r}_0
\end{align*}
Additionally, since the choice for \(\underline{r}_0\) is mostly arbitrary we are left us with the differential form of Gauss's law:
\[\dive{E}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho\]
This is the first of the Maxwell equations and holds for all points where \(\rho\) is continuous and \(\dive{E}\) is continuously differentiable. Notice if \(\rho=0\) then this tells us that the divergence of the electric field is \(0\) which makes sense intuitively since there are no points that could generate a charge. Gauss's law allows us to find the flux through a "reasonable" surface as long as the volume it incloses is compact.
\[\Phi=\oint_{\partial \mathcal{V}}\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=\int_{\mathcal{V}}\dive{E}\,\text{d}\tau=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\rho(\underline{r})\,\text{d}\tau=\frac{q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}\]
This is called the integral form of Gauss's law.
\subsection{The curl of \(\underline{E}\)}
Now that we have examined the integral of the divergence of the field, we may wish to consider what happens when we integrate the curl. Consider the line integral of \(\underline{E}\) around a circle:
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{0})}\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{r}\]
Note that we can make some simplifications (such as setting the centre of our disc to be \(\underline{0}\) since the choice is arbitrary). When we integrate \(\underline{E}\) generated by a point charge around any circle we get \(0\). Hence by Stokes' theorem:
\[\int_{\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{r}_0)}(\dive{E})\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}=0\]
Since the disc is in the \(x-y\) plane we have that \(\hat{\underline{n}}=\hat{\underline{z}}\) and:
\[(\curl{E})\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}=(\curl{E})\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a=(\curl{E})_z\,\text{d}a\]
Then:
\[\int_{\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{r}_0)}(\curl{E})_z\,\text{d}a=0\]
From the mean value theorem of integration there exists \(\underline{r}_1\in\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{r}_0)\) such that:
\[\int_{\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{r}_0)}(\curl{E})_z\,\text{d}a=|\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{r}_0)|(\curl{E})_z(\underline{r}_1)=0\]
Since \(|\mathcal{D}_R(\underline{r}_0)|\ne 0\) we require:
\[(\curl{E})_z(\underline{r}_1)=0\]
If we take the limit as \(R\to 0\):
\[\lim_{R\to 0}\underline{r}_1=\underline{r}_0\]
and:
\[(\curl{E})_z(\underline{r}_0)\]
But since \(\underline{r}_0\) is a free choice so we have that:
\[(\curl{E})_z=0\]
everywhere. We can repeat this argument for the other planes and see that:
\[\curl{E}=\underline{0}\]
This is a fundamental law of electrostatics but it doesn't have a name. Note that this law only exists for electrostatics, when we have magnetic fields or dynamic fields then we need to alter this equation. The above is the equation in differential form, in integral form it is:
\[\oint_{\partial S}\underline{E}\cdot \,\text{d}\underline{r}=0\]
\subsection{Applications and Potential}
The above law manes that the electric field is conservative. For an conservative field we can express it as the gradient of a scalar field:
\[\underline{E}=\grad{\phi}\]
Where \(\phi\) is a scalar potential function. If \(\underline{E}\) is simply connected the we can derive this from \(\curl{E}=\underline{0}\). In electromagnetism it is conventional to introduce:
\[V(\underline{r})=-\phi(\underline{r})\]
We call \(V(\underline{r})\) the electrostatic potential. The potential \(V\) is only defined up to an added constant:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{r})=-\grad{V(\underline{r})}=-\grad{(V(\underline{r})+V_0)}\]
We calculate the potential by choosing some point \(\underline{r}_0\) and integrating the field from there:
\[V(\underline{r})=-\int^{\underline{r}}_{\underline{r}_0}\underline{E}(\underline{\tilde{r}})\cdot\text{d}\underline{\tilde{r}}\]
\subsubsection{Example - Potential of a point charge}
Consider a point charge \(q\) at the origin. We have:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{\tilde{r}})=\frac{q\underline{\tilde{r}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \tilde{r}^3}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\tilde{r}^2}\underline{\hat{\tilde{r}}}\]
We choose a starting point \(\underline{r}_0\), then we can construct a sphere of radius \(r_0\) centred at the origin where clearly \(\underline{r}_0\) lies on it's surface. We can define a curve parameter \(t\):
\[\underline{r}_0\in \partial \mathcal{B}_{r_0}(\mathcal{O})\]
\[\df{\underline{\tilde{r}}}{t}\perp \tilde{r}\]
Therefore the integrand for the part of the line integral going from the origin to the point \(r_0\underline{\hat{r}}\) is 0. This means that \(V\) is constant on the sphere of radius \(\underline{r}_0\) and:
\[V(r_0\underline{\hat{r}})=V(\underline{r}_0)\]
Now to find the last part of the line integral we can use \(\tilde{r}\) as a curve parameter:
\[\underline{\tilde{r}}=\tilde{r}\underline{\hat{r}}\]
\[\df{\underline{\tilde{r}}}{\tilde{r}}=\underline{\hat{r}}\]
Then:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{\tilde{r}})=\frac{q\underline{\tilde{r}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\tilde{r}^2}\]
\[V(\underline{r})=-\frac{-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^{\underline{r}}_{\underline{r}_0}\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^2}\,\text{d}\tilde{r}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_0}\]
But \(V\) is only defined up to a constant so it's usual to drop the second term:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\]
This is called Coulombs' law for potentials. If the charge \(q\) is at \(\underline{r}'\):
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\scriptr}\]
If we have charges \(q_j\) at points \(\underline{r}_j'\) then:
\[V(\underline{r})=\sum_j\frac{q_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\scriptr_j}\]
and in the case of a continuous charge distribution \(\rho\):
\[V(\underline{r})=\int\frac{\rho(\underline{r}')}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\scriptr}\text{d}\tau'\]
\subsubsection{Some remarks}
At the start of the module we learnt that:
\[\underline{F}=\underline{E}Q\]
for a test charge \(Q\).
\[W=Q\int^{\underline{r}}_{\underline{r}_0}\underline{E}(\underline{\tilde{r}})\cdot\text{d}\underline{\tilde{r}}\]
This quantity is called work. \(QV\) is called the potential energy of the test charge \(Q\) in the electric field. We can write gauss' law in terms of the potential:
\[\dive{E}=-\underline{\nabla}\cdot\grad{V}=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\]
\[\Delta V=\nabla^2V=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\]
This is called the Poisson equation. If we are at a point where \(\rho=0\) we have:
\[\nabla^2V=0\]
Which is called the Laplace equation.
\subsection{Electric Dipole Example}
Consider an electric dipole with a charge \(q\) at \(\underline{r}_+=R\underline{\hat{z}}\) and a charge \(-q\) at \(r_-=-R\underline{\hat{z}}\). The potential of this is:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}_+|}-\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}_-|}\right)\]
We are going to use spherical coordinates:
\[\underline{r}-\underline{r}_{\pm}=r\sin\theta\cos\phi \underline{\hat{x}}+r\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+(r\cos\theta\mp R)\]
\[|\underline{r}-\underline{r}_{\pm}|^2=r^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\phi+r^2\sin^2\theta\sin^2\phi+(r\cos\theta\mp R)^2\]
\[=r^2\sin^2\theta+r^2\cos^2\theta+R^2\mp 2Rr\cos\theta\]
\[=r^2+R^2\mp 2Rr\cos\theta\]
The potential is now:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+R^2-2Rr\cos\theta}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+R^2+2Rr\cos\theta}}\right)\]
Consider \(\frac{R}{r}\ll 1\):
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{R^2}{r^2}-2\frac{R}{r}\cos\theta}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{R^2}{r^2}+2\frac{R}{r}\cos\theta}}\right)\]
Introduce \(u=\frac{R}{r}\):
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^2-2u\cos\theta}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^2+2u\cos\theta}}\right)\]
Since \(u\) is so small we can do a Maclaurin expansion at \(u=0\):
\[\df{V}{u}=-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\left(\frac{2u-2\cos\theta}{2(1+u^2-2u\cos\theta)^{\frac{3}{2}}}-\frac{2u+2\cos\theta}{2(1+u^2+2u\cos\theta)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)\]
\[V|_{u=0}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\left(\frac{1}{1}-\frac{1}{1}\right)=0\]
\[\df{V}{u}|_{u=0}=-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\left(-\cos\theta-\cos\theta\right)=\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}\cos\theta\]
Then:
\[V\approx0+\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}u\cos\theta\]
\[V\approx\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}R\cos\theta=\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}Rr\cos\theta\]
However note that this last term looks like a dot product of a two vectors. Introduce \(\underline{d}=\underline{r}_+-\underline{r}_-\) where \(\underline{d}\) is the distance between the charges. Then notice that \(R=\frac{1}{2}|\underline{d}|\) and \(Rr\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}\underline{d}\cdot\underline{r}\):
\[V(\underline{r})\approx\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\underline{d}\cdot\underline{r}\]
Now we can introduce \(\underline{p}=q\underline{d}\), the "dipole moment". Finally we can write our potential in terms of this dipole moment:
\[V(\underline{r})\approx\frac{\underline{p}\cdot\underline{r}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}=\frac{\underline{p}\cdot\underline{\hat{r}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\]
In general these charges can go wherever we want and are not forced to be on the \(z-\)axis so we can introduce \(\underline{r}'\) as the midpoint of the dipole and:
\[\underline{\scriptr}=\underline{r}-\underline{r}'\]
\[V(\underline{r})\approx\frac{\underline{p}\cdot\underline{\hat{\scriptr}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\scriptr^2}\]
That was a lot of work but finally we have a form of the potential that was can reasonably use as a good enough approximation.
\subsection{Electric Fields in Matter}
So far we have only considered electric fields in a vacuum. In this module we will consider dielectric matter which contains atoms of a positive charge nucleus \(q\), surrounded by an electron cloud of negative charge \(-q\). The total charge of the atom is \(0\). If an external electric field is present then the cloud centre shifts slightly, which creates a dipole between the centre and the nucleus of the atom. This dipole has a dipole potential \(\underline{p}\). As we have just seen the additional potential present is:
\[V_{1-atom}(\underline{r})=\frac{\underline{p}\cdot\underline{\hat{\scriptr}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\scriptr^2}\]
As our dielectric material almost certainly contains multiple atoms:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_j\frac{\underline{p}_j\cdot\underline{\scriptr}_j}{\scriptr^2_j}\]
As the number of atoms in a given material sample is very large we can approximate the sum using an integral:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_\mathcal{V}\frac{\underline{P}(\underline{r}')\cdot\underline{\scriptr}}{\scriptr^2}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
Where \(\mathcal{V}\) is the volume containing our dielectric material. \(\underline{P}\) is called the polarisation density. Let \(\underline{\nabla}'=\left(\partial/\partial x',\, \partial/\partial y',\,\partial/\partial z'\right)\), then:
\[\underline{\nabla}'\left(\frac{1}{\scriptr}\right)=\frac{\underline{\scriptr}}{\scriptr^3}\]
and:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_\mathcal{V}\underline{\nabla}'\cdot\left(\frac{\underline{P}}{\scriptr}\right)\,\text{d}\tau'-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\frac{1}{\scriptr}\underline{\nabla'}\cdot\underline{P}(\underline{r}')\,\text{d}\tau'\]
We can simplify this using the divergence theorem:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}}\frac{\underline{P}\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}}{\scriptr}\,\text{d}a'-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\frac{1}{\scriptr}\underline{\nabla'}\cdot\underline{P}(\underline{r}')\,\text{d}\tau'\]
This potential is equivalent to:
\[V(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}}\frac{\sigma_b(\underline{r}')}{\scriptr}\,\text{d}a'+\int_\mathcal{V}\frac{\rho_b(\underline{r}')}{\scriptr}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
with the bound surface density:
\[\sigma_b=\underline{P}\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}\]
and the bound charge density:
\[\rho_b=-\underline{\nabla}\cdot\underline{P}=-\underline{\nabla}'\cdot\underline{P}(\underline{r}')\]
Then away from the boundary we have a total charge density:
\[\rho=\rho_f+\rho_b\]
Where \(\rho_f\) is the free charge density that generates the field. The electric field still satisfies Gauss' law but now with the total charge density:
\[\underline{\nabla}\cdot(\varepsilon_0\underline{E}+\underline{P})=\rho_f\]
We write;
\[\underline{D}=\varepsilon_0\underline{E}+\underline{P}\]
which is called the electric displacement and Gauss' law is:
\[\underline{\nabla}\cdot\underline{D}=\rho_f\]
\subsection{What is \(\rho\)?}
In many materials, the relationship between the polarisation and the electric field is linear:
\[\underline{P}=\varepsilon_0\chi_{e}\underline{E}\]
Where \(\chi_e\) is called the electric susceptibility and depends on the material. in a question this value may be given to you if required. Then:
\[\underline{D}=\varepsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\underline{E}\]
This quantity \(1+\chi_e\) is called the relative permittivity or dielectric constant.

\section{Magnetostatics}
\subsection{Introduction}
Consider a wire as a curve in \(\mathbb{R^3}\). Wires are typically made of from conductors - the current, \(I\), through a point on the ave is the charge that flows through it per unit time and is measured in amp\`eres:
\[1\text{A}=1\frac{\text{C}}{\text{s}}\]
If you place a magnetic compass near a wire with charge flowing through it, then the compass points tangent to a circle around the wire. This motivates the magnetic field \(\underline{B}\) pointing in the direction of the magnetic compass. Now consider a wire with a current through it and a test charge parallel to the wire. Then the magnetic field is such that:
\[\underline{F}_{mag}=Q\underline{v}\times \underline{B}\]
This force is called the Lorentz force and it obeys a right-hand rule. Then the total force acting on \(Q\) is:
\[\underline{F}=Q(\underline{E}+\underline{v}\times\underline{B})\]
\subsection{Biot-Savart Law}
The Lorentz force defines the field \(B\). What we would like to know is what the magnetic field generated by a steady state current \(I\) is. Consider an infinitesimal piece of wire \(\text{d}\underline{r}'\) on a curve \(\underline{r}'\) with:
\[\text{d}\underline{r}'=\df{\underline{r}'}{t}\text{d}t\]
Physicists will tell us that the contribution of such a piece of wire to the magnetic field is:
\[d\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{\text{d}\underline{r}'\times(\underline{r}-\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|^3}\]
The constant \(\mu_0\) is called the permeability of free space and its value is:
\[\mu_0\approx4\pi\times 10^{-7}\frac{\text{N}}{\text{A}^2}\]
If our thin wire runs along a curve \(C\) we can integrate \(d\underline{B}\) along it to get:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_C\frac{\text{d}\underline{r}'\times(\underline{r}-\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|^3}\]
This is called the Biot-Savart law. This integral looks a little weird with a vector product in the integrand so consider the parametrised version:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int^{t_1}_{t_0}\frac{\df{\underline{r}'}{t}\times(\underline{r}-\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}t\]
\subsubsection{Example}
Consider an infinitely long straight wire. We choose coordinate systems such that the wire runs along the \(z\)-axis and the test point \(\underline{r}\) is on the \(x\)-axis. Then:
\[\underline{r}'=z'\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{r}=r\underline{\hat{x}}\]
\[\df{\underline{r}'}{z'}=\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\df{\underline{r}'}{z'}\times(\underline{r}-\underline{r}')=\det\begin{pmatrix}
    \underline{\hat{x}} &\underline{\hat{y}} & \underline{\hat{z}} \\
    0 & 0& 1 \\
    r & 0 & z'
\end{pmatrix}=r\underline{\hat{y}}\]
We can now compute the integral:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0Ir}{4\pi}\int^\infty_{-\infty}\frac{1}{(r^2+z'^2)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}z'\ \underline{\hat{y}}\]
\[\implies \underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\underline{\hat{y}}\]
If we had a test charge \(Q\), moving in the \(x-y\) plane with velocity \(\underline{v}=v\underline{\hat{z}}\), then the resulting Lorentz force would be:
\[F_{mag}=Q\underline{v}\times \underline{B}=\frac{\mu_0IQv}{2\pi r}\underline{\hat{z}}\times\underline{\hat{y}}=-\frac{\mu_0IQv}{2\pi r}\underline{\hat{x}}\]
\subsection{Force Between Two Wires}
The above formulation allows us to find the force between two infinitely long thin wires. Consider a wire on \(\underline{z}\) with current \(I_1\) and a second wire, parallel to the first at distance \(r\), with current of \(I_2\). From physics we know that the line charge of free electrons is \(\lambda_-\) and the remaining nuclei and bounded charges are \(\lambda_+\). If the wire is electrically neutral then:
\[\lambda_-=-\lambda_+\]
If we the electrons move with velocity \(-\underline{v}=-v\underline{\hat{z}}\), then the current in the second wire is:
\[I_2=-\lambda_-v=\lambda_+v\]
in the positive \(z\) direction. This allows us to find the force per unit length on the second wire:
\[f_{mag}=\frac{\mu_0I_1\lambda_+v}{2\pi r}\underline{\hat{z}}\times \underline{\hat{y}}=-\frac{\mu_0I_1I_2}{2\pi r}\underline{\hat{x}}\]
\subsection{Current Density}
Assuming a wire is a line is usually a good approximation but in general we might have charges that move around. We wish to introduce a current density \(\underline{J}\). The current density is defined such that for any piecewise smooth surface, the current through the surface is:
\[I=\int_S\underline{J}\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}\]
Assuming our surface is a sphere, then:
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\underline{J}\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}\]
Is the current through the sphere: Essentially the charge flowing out per unit time. Physicists tell us that electric charge is conserved, so the total charge must decrease by the amount leaving through the surface. Then:
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\underline{J}\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}=-\pf{}{t}q_{enc}\]
Using the divergence theorem:
\[\int_{\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\dive{J}\,\text{d}\tau=-\pf{}{t}\int_{\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\rho\,\text{d}\tau\]
Like we did before for electric field, we can apply the mean value theorem of integration here and get:
\[\dive{J}=-\pf{\rho}{t}\]
This is a continuity equation. For differentiable current densities we can use the above result to generalise the Biot-Savart law:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')\times\underline{\scriptr}}{\scriptr^3}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
Like before, we are now interested in considering the divergence and curl of \(\underline{B}\).
\subsection{The Vector Potential}
Lets reformulate the Biot-Savart law using:
\[\underline{\nabla}\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}=\frac{\underline{r}-\underline{r}'}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|^3}\]
\[\implies \underline{B}(\underline{r})=-\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int \underline{J}(\underline{r}')\times\underline{\nabla}\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
However notice that this integrand is a vector product of a vector field and a gradient field. Using standard results from the formula sheet:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\left[\underline{\nabla}\times\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}-\frac{\underline{\nabla}\times\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\right]\,\text{d}\tau'\]
But \(\curl{J}=\underline{0}\) because \(\underline{J}\) only depends on \(\underline{r}'\).
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\underline{\nabla}\times\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
If the integrand is sufficiently well behaved (Continuous and falls off sufficiently fast as \(\underline{r}'\to\infty\)) for all integrals to exist then we can take the curl out of the integrand:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\underline{\nabla}\times\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
We call this second term the vector potential of \(\underline{B}\) and label it \(\underline{A}\).
\[\underline{B}=\curl{A}\]
Note that the vector potential is not unique and in particular:
\[\underline{\nabla}\times(A+\grad{f})=\curl{A}=\underline{B}\]
\subsection{Curl and Divergence of the Magnetic Field}
\subsubsection{Curl}
We start with Biot-Savart in a suitable form:
\[\underline{B}(\underline{r})=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\underline{\nabla}\times\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
Using the result:
\[\underline{\nabla}\times(\curl{A})=\underline{\nabla}(\dive{A})-\underline{\nabla}^2\underline{A}\]
We can write the curl of \(\underline{B}\) as:
\[\curl{B}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\underline{\nabla}\int\underline{\nabla}\cdot\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'-\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\underline{\nabla}^2\int\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
\[=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\underline{\nabla}\int \underline{J}\cdot\underline{\nabla}\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'-\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\underline{\nabla}^2\int\frac{\underline{J}(\underline{r}')}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\,\text{d}\tau'\]
Furthermore we have the results:
\[\underline{\nabla}\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}=-\underline{\nabla}'\frac{1}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|}\]
and,
\[\underline{J}\cdot\underline{\nabla}'\frac{1}{\scriptr}=\frac{1}{\scriptr}\left(J_x\pf{}{x'}+J_y\pf{}{y'}+J_z\pf{}{z'}\right)\]
\[=\underline{\nabla}'\cdot\left(\frac{\underline{J}}{\scriptr}\right)-(\underline{\nabla}'\cdot\underline{J})\frac{1}{\scriptr}\]
