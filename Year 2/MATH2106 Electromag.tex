\section{Electrostatics}
\subsection{A Word on Notation}
Most of electromagnetism takes place in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), although unless we are taking a vector product, there is no reason for it to not apply in higher dimensions. Vectors should always be underlined and in a piece of standard physics notation, the same character used but not underlined represents the magnitude of that vector:
\[r=|\underline{r}|\]
Unit vectors are denoted with a hat:
\[\underline{r}=r\,\underline{\hat{r}}\]
Most of electromagnetism will take place in Cartesian coordinates. The Cartesian unit vectors are:
\[\underline{\hat{x}},\ \underline{\hat{y}},\ \underline{\hat{z}}\]
\subsection{Charges}
The SI unit of charge is Coulombs (C). These are defined such that the charge of the electron is:
\[q_e=-e=-1.60\dots\times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}\]
You will not be asked to remember this. In these lectures, a small \(q\) will be used for charges that generate electric fields and a capital \(Q\) will be used for a "test charge" that has forces acting upon it from existing in a field. We are generally interested in the force caused by \(q\) and acting on \(Q\). This force is given to us via Coulombs law:
\[\underline{F}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{\scriptr^2}\hat{\underline{\scriptr}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}'|^3}(\underline{r}-\underline{r}')\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/singlechargeforce.png}
    \caption{A test charge \(Q\) is acted on by a field generated from \(q\).}
\end{figure}
\subsubsection{Observations of Coulombs law}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The magnitude of the force decreases quadratically with the distance between the two charges.
    \item Force is proportional to the product of the charges.
    \item If \(q\) and \(Q\) have the same sign, then the force points away from the field. This is repulsion.
    \item If \(q\) and \(Q\) have opposite signs, then the force points towards the field. This is attraction.
    \item \(\varepsilon_0\) is the vacuum permittivity and is approximately:
    \[\varepsilon_0\approx 8.854\times 10^{-12}\,\frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{Nm}^2} \]
    You do not need to memorise this.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Multiple Charges}
If the force acting on \(Q\) is generated by \(n\) charges \(q_i\), then it is equal to the sum of the forces by the individual charges:
\[\underline{F}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum^n_{i=1}\frac{q_iQ}{|\underline{r}-\underline{r}_i'|^3}(\underline{r}-\underline{r}_i')\]
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/multiplechargeforce.png}
    \caption{A test charge \(Q\), acted on by force generated from multiple charges \(q_i\).}
\end{figure}
Notice that we can take \(Q\) out of the sum and write:
\[\underline{F}=Q\underline{E}\]
Where \(\underline{E}(\underline{r})\) is called the electric field and is defined as:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum^n_{i=1}\frac{q_i}{\scriptr^2}\hat{\underline{\scriptr}}\]
\subsection{Continuous Charge Distributions}
Coulombs law doesn't restrict us to just working with discrete sums (although in reality electrons are point charges). In particular if we have a field with lots of charges we may need to look at continuous charge distributions:
\[\underline{E}(\underline{r})=\int\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{\scriptr^2}\hat{\underline{\scriptr}}\,\text{d}q\]
The actual form of this integral will depend on where the charges are located. For example we could have a line or surface integral. If the charge is concentrated on a curve, then define the charge density \(\lambda\):
\[\text{d}q=\lambda \text{d}\ell\]
In this case \(\ell\) is some length parameter along the curve.
\subsubsection{Line charges}
We can construct the electric field for a continuous charge distributions where our charges form a line. Notice that the force acting on a test charge from this line decreases with distance and indeed if the distance from the line is significantly larger than the line itself then it just appears like a point charge at the origin.
\subsubsection{Spherical coordinate recap}
Recall the definitions for spherical coordinates:
\begin{align*}
    &x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi \\
    &y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi \\
    &z=r\cos\theta
\end{align*}
Where:
\begin{align*}
    &\theta\in[0,\pi] && \text{is the polar angle} \\
    &\phi\in [0,2\pi) && \text{is the azimuthal angle}
\end{align*}
Assume we wish to integrate a function \(f\) over a volume \(V\):
\[\int_V f(\underline{r})\,\text{d}\tau\]
In Cartesian coordinates this volume element is:
\[\text{d}\tau=\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}z\]
In spherical coordinates the volume element is:
\[\text{d}\tau=r^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
Consider a surface integral of a sphere of radius \(R\):
\[\int_Sf(\underline{r})\,\text{d}a\]
\[\text{d}a=R^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
The surface area is calculated as:
\[A=\int_S\text{d}a=\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0R^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[=2\pi R^2\int^\pi_0\sin\theta\,\text{d}\theta=4\pi R^2\]
Likewise we can calculate the volume of a ball of radius \(R\):
\[V=\int_B\,\text{d}\tau=\int^R_0\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0r^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}\phi\]
\[=4\pi\int^R_0r^2\,\text{d}r=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\]
\subsubsection{Charges on the surface of a sphere}
Consider a sphere of radius \(R\) with a uniform surface charge \(\sigma\). We wish to know the field at a distance \(r\) from the centre of the sphere. We can choose our axis such that:
\[\underline{r}=r\underline{\hat{z}}\]
A point on the sphere has position vector:
\[r'=R\sin\theta'\cos\phi'\underline{\hat{x}}+R\sin\theta'\sin\phi'\underline{\hat{y}}+R\cos\theta'\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}=\underline{r}-\underline{r}'=-R\sin\theta'\cos\phi'\underline{\hat{x}}-R\sin\theta'\sin\phi'\underline{\hat{y}}+(r-R\cos\theta')\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\scriptr^2=\underline{\scriptr}\cdot\underline{\scriptr}=R^2+r^2-2rR\cos\theta'\]
By symmetry of \(\sin\phi'\) and \(\cos\phi'\) we have that:
\begin{align*}
    E_x=0 && E_y=0
\end{align*}
Finally we need to compute \(E_z\), which is the most tedious integration of the entire module (apparently).
\[E_z=\frac{\sigma R^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0\frac{(r-R\cos\theta')\sin\theta'}{(R^2+r^2-2rR\cos\theta')^{3/2}}\,\text{d}\phi'\,\text{d}\theta'\]
We can make a substitution to make this integral easier to solve:
\begin{align*}
    &u=\cos\theta' && \text{d}u=-\sin\theta'\,\text{d}\theta' \\
    &u(0)=1 && u(\pi)=1
\end{align*}
Then:
\[E_z=\frac{-\sigma R^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\int^{-1}_1\frac{r-Ru}{(R^2+r^2-2rRu)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}u\]
\[E_z=\frac{\sigma R(r^2-R^2)}{4\varepsilon_0 r^2}\left(\frac{1}{|r-R|}-\frac{1}{r+R}\right)-\frac{\sigma R^2}{4\varepsilon_0 r^2}(|r-R|+(r+R))\]
Consider the case \(r>R\):
\[E_z=\frac{\sigma R^2}{\varepsilon_0 r^2}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\]
And since the charge generating the field is spherically symmetric, then:
\[\underline{E}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\underline{r}\]
This means that outside the sphere, the field is exactly that of a point charge at the origin. if \(r<R\) then:
\[E_z\implies \underline{E}=\underline{0}\]
Inside the sphere, the electric charge is 0.
\subsubsection{General spherically symmetric charge densities}
If we have a spherically symmetric charge density \(\rho\) on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), that is that \(\rho\) depends only on \(r'\). Then:
\[\underline{E}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^\infty_0\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0\rho(r')\frac{\underline{\scriptr}}{\scriptr^{3/2}}r'^2\sin\theta'\,\text{d}\phi'\,\text{d}\theta'\,\text{d}r'\]
As in the case of the sphere surface charge:
\[E_x=E_y=0\]
\[E_z=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 r^3}\int^r_0\rho(r')r'^2\,\text{d}r'\,\underline{r}\]
What this is saying is that the field generated by a spherically symmetric charge distribution \(r\) from the centre of the distribution is the same as that generated by a point charge at the centre, with magnitude contained inside the ball of radius \(r\). The charge outside of the radius \(r\) has no influence on the field at \(r\).
\subsection{Electric Flux}
Graphically we can represent an electric field with field lines. These are tangent lines to the field at every point in space. we can imagine these field lines passing through a surface and this is defined to be the flux of the field across the surface:
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Year 2/Images/Fieldlines.png}
    \caption{Field lines passing through a surface.}
\end{figure}
We can choose a unit normal \(\underline{\hat{n}}\) to our surface \(S\). We can define the flux of a vector field \(\underline{E}\) through a surface \(S\) as:
\[\Phi=\int_S\underline{E}\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a\]
\subsubsection{Some remarks}
\begin{itemize}
    \item It is typical to write \(\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a\) as \(\text{d}\underline{a}\).
    \item There are two choices for normal vectors and choosing between them means choosing in what direction flux is counted as positive.
    \item This formula only holds for surfaces where normal vectors can be found. This includes smooth and piecewise smooth surfaces. If a surface is piecewise smooth you can calculate the flux by summing all of the individual fluxes through the faces.
    \item The surface must be orientable.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection*{Example}
Find the flux of the electric field generated by a point charge at the origin, through a circular disc of radius \(R\) at heigh \(z=h>0\).
\[\underline{r}'=\underline{0}\implies \underline{\scriptr}=\underline{r}\]
\[\underline{r}=r\cos\theta\underline{\hat{x}}+r\sin\theta\underline{\hat{y}}+h\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\scriptr^2=r^2+h^2\]
By coulomb's law:
\[\underline{E}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{\scriptr^3}\underline{\scriptr}\]
Choose \(\underline{\hat{n}}=\underline{\hat{z}}\) to be the outward normal. Then:
\[\text{d}\underline{a}=\underline{\hat{n}}\,\text{d}a=\underline{\hat{z}}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}r\]
Now we can find the flux:
\[\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=E_z\,\text{d}a\]
\[\Phi=\int_S\underline{E}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^R_0\int^{2\pi}_0\frac{h}{(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}r\,\text{d}\theta\,\text{d}r\]
\[=\frac{hq}{2\varepsilon_0}\int^R_0\frac{r}{(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}r\]
\[=\frac{-hq}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(r^2+h^2)^{1/2}}\right]^{r=R}_{r=0}\]
\[=\frac{hq}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{h}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+h^2}}\right)\]
\subsection{Volumes and Closed Surfaces}
Lets introduce some new notation. An arbitrarily shaped volume in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) will be called \(\mathcal{V}\) and it's surface \(\partial \mathcal{V}\). We will assume that \(\partial \mathcal{V}\) is piecewise smooth. The volume of \(\mathcal{V}\) will be denoted \(|\mathcal{V}|\). A particularly useful volume is the ball of radius \(R\) centred around \(\underline{r}_0\): We call this ball \(\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\) and it's surface \(\partial\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)\). The flux through a closed surface us often written using a circulation integral:
\[\Phi=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}}\underline{E}\cdot\text{d}\underline{a}\]
It is convention to choose \(\underline{\hat{n}}\) facing outwards.
\subsection{Relating Flux and Charge}
Consider a point charge \(q\) generating a volume field  and its flux through a sphere of radius \(R\). We choose the coordinates such that the charge is on the \(z\) axis and the sphere is centred at the origin. We can choose \(q\) to be either inside or outside the sphere.
\[\underline{r}'=r'\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{r}=R\sin\theta\cos\phi\underline{\hat{x}}+R\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+R\cos\theta\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}=R\sin\theta\cos\phi\underline{\hat{x}}+R\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+(R\cos\theta-r')\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\scriptr^2=R^2+r'^2-2Rr'\cos\theta\]
\[\underline{\hat{n}}=\underline{\hat{r}}=\sin\theta\cos\phi\underline{\hat{x}}+\sin\theta\sin\phi\underline{\hat{y}}+\cos\theta\underline{\hat{z}}\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}\cdot\underline{\hat{n}}=R-r'\cos\theta\]
\[\underline{\scriptr}\cdot \text{d}\underline{a}=(R-r'\cos\theta)R^2\sin\theta\,\text{d}\phi\,\text{d}\theta\]
\[\implies \Phi=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int^\pi_0\int^{2\pi}_0\frac{(R-r'\cos\theta)R^2\sin\theta}{(R^2+r'^2-2Rr'\cos\theta)^{3/2}}\,\text{d}\phi\,\text{d}\theta\]
\[\implies\Phi=\begin{cases}
    \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} & r'<R \\
    0 & r'>R
\end{cases}\]
If we instead have several point charges generating the field we get:
\[\Phi=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\sum_{j,\underline{r}_j\in \mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r_0)}}q_j\]
This looks much more complicated than it is. All this is saying is to find the total flux we just sum the all the charges inside the ball of radius \(R\). This means that charges outside the ball do not affect the flux. We usually use some shorthand notation to make this easier to write:
\[\Phi=\frac{q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}\]
Where the subscript represents all the charges enclosed. This statement still holds if the charge distribution is continuous:
\[q_{enc}=\int_{\mathcal{B}_R(\underline{r}_0)}\rho(\underline{r}')\,\text{d}\tau'\]
\subsection{Flux Through Arbitrary Closed Surfaces}
Recall the mean value theorem for integration: If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is a continuous function, there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) such that:
\[\int^b_af(x)\,\text{d}x=(b-a)f(c)\]
